Marine Insurance — Comprehensive Lecture (Marine Insurance Act, 1963)
The Most Important Distinction in Indian Insurance Law

Marine insurance has the special distinction of being the ONLY branch of insurance in India governed by a comprehensive dedicated statute — the Marine Insurance Act, 1963 (Act No. 11 of 1963 · 91 Sections + Schedule). All other principles of insurance law — utmost good faith, insurable interest, indemnity, subrogation, contribution, proximate cause, warranties — were first articulated and refined in marine insurance and then extended to other branches.

Modelled on the Marine Insurance Act, 1906 (England) — drafted by Sir Mackenzie Chalmers and described by Hardy Ivamy as "an extremely thorough and concise piece of work."

§ 3
Definition — Section 3, Marine Insurance Act, 1963
Section 3 — Marine Insurance Defined

"A contract of marine insurance is an agreement whereby the insurer undertakes to indemnify the assured, in the manner and to the extent thereby agreed, against marine losses, that is to say, the losses incidental to marine adventure."

Key Definitions — Section 2

§§ 6–17
Insurable Interest in Marine Insurance
Section 7(2) — Insurable Interest Defined

"A person is interested in a marine adventure where he stands in any legal or equitable relation to the adventure or to any insurable property at risk therein, in consequence of which he may benefit by the safety or due arrival of insurable property, or may be prejudiced by its loss, or by damage thereto, or by the detention thereof, or may incur liability in respect thereof."

Section 8 — When Insurable Interest Must Exist — Critical Rule

§ 8(1): The assured must be interested in the subject matter insured at the time of the loss, though he need not be interested when the insurance is effected.
Critical Exception (Proviso): Where subject matter is insured "lost or not lost" — assured may recover although interest not acquired until after the loss, UNLESS at time of effecting the contract, the assured was aware of the loss and the insurer was not.
§ 8(2): Where the assured has no interest at time of loss, he cannot acquire interest by any act or election after he is aware of the loss.

Contrast with life insurance: Life = insurable interest at commencement only (Dalby). Marine = must exist at time of loss (§ 8) — aligned with nature as contract of indemnity.

⚖ Anderson v. Morice — INSURABLE INTEREST TIMING

A insured a shipload of rice from Rangoon. Policy stated "interest as may appear by ship Sunbeam." Peril occurred while loading was still in progress. Held: A had NO insurable interest at time of loss — his risk was to commence only after the ship was fully loaded (per policy terms). Loading was incomplete — risk had not yet attached.

Utmost Good Faith — Disclosure & Representations
SectionRule
§ 19"A contract of marine insurance is a contract based upon the utmost good faith, and if the utmost good faith be not observed by either party, the contract may be avoided by the other party."
§ 20(1)Assured must disclose to insurer, before contract is concluded, every material circumstance known to him. Assured deemed to know every circumstance which, in ordinary course of business, ought to be known to him. Failure = insurer may avoid.

§ 20(3)Need NOT be disclosed in absence of inquiry: (a) any circumstance which diminishes the risk; (b) any circumstance known or presumed to be known to insurer; (c) any circumstance as to which information is waived; (d) any circumstance superfluous due to express/implied warranty.

§ 21Agent effecting insurance must disclose: (a) every material circumstance known to himself (agent deemed to know what ought to be known in ordinary course); (b) every material circumstance the assured is bound to disclose.
§ 22(1)&(2)Every material representation during negotiations before contract concluded must be true — if untrue, insurer may avoid. Materiality = would influence judgment of prudent insurer.
§ 22(4)Representation as to fact = true if substantially correct. Representation as to expectation/belief = true if made in good faith.
§ 22(6)A representation may be withdrawn or corrected before the contract is concluded.
§ 23Contract deemed concluded when proposal of assured is accepted by insurer, whether policy issued or not. Reference may be made to the slip, covering note or customary memorandum — even if unstamped.

§§ 35–43
Warranties in Marine Insurance — The Strictest Doctrine
The Critical Distinction — Insurance Law vs. Contract Law

General Contract Law: A warranty is a minor term — breach gives rise to damages only, NOT right to terminate.

Marine Insurance Law: A warranty is a condition of the UTMOST STRICTNESS. § 35(3): "A warranty is a condition which must be exactly complied with, whether it be material to the risk or not. If it be not so complied with, then, subject to any express provision in the policy, the insurer is discharged from liability as from the date of the breach of warranty, but without prejudice to any liability incurred by him before that date."

📦
§ 42 — No Implied Warranty that Goods are Seaworthy
▾

§ 42(1): In a policy on goods — there is NO implied warranty that goods are seaworthy.
§ 42(2): In a voyage policy on goods — there IS an implied warranty that at commencement of the voyage the SHIP is: (a) seaworthy as a ship; AND (b) reasonably fit to carry the goods to the destination (cargo-worthy).

⚖ Blackett v. National Benefit Assurance Co. — CARGO-WORTHINESS

Cotton was insured. Some quantity lost due to maritime peril. Insurer contended the vessel faced turbulent conditions because cotton stacked in the hold was too light. Court rejected this — insurer held liable. There is no implied warranty on the goods; only the ship needs to be seaworthy and cargo-worthy.

§ 43 — Warranty of Legality (Implied)

"There is an implied warranty that the adventure insured is a lawful one, and that, so far as the assured can control the matter, the adventure shall be carried out in a lawful manner." Policy on smuggling voyage, piratical adventure, or trade with enemy = void.

⚖ Cory v. Burr — BARRATRY + WARRANTY OF LEGALITY

Ship seized by Spanish authorities because master committed smuggling. Insurer not liable. Established: (1) Barratry by smuggling is a valid legal principle. (2) Smuggling violates the implied warranty of legality. (3) Barratry requires wrongful act by master/crew AGAINST the interest of the owner — if owner orders it, not barratry.

§§ 44–51
The Voyage — Commencement, Deviation & Delay
SectionRule
§ 44Commencement of Risk: Implied condition that adventure commenced within reasonable time. If not — insurer may avoid. Condition negatived if delay known to insurer before contract, or waived.
§ 45Alteration of Port of Departure: If ship sails from a different place from that specified — risk does NOT attach.
§ 46Sailing for Different Destination: If ship sails to a different destination from that specified — risk does NOT attach.
§ 47Change of Voyage: Where after commencement of risk, destination is voluntarily changed — insurer discharged from time of change (when determination to change is manifested) — even if ship has not yet left original course.
§ 48(1)Deviation: Where ship, without lawful excuse, deviates from voyage contemplated — insurer discharged from liability from time of deviation — even if ship regained her route before loss.
§ 48(3) ★"The intention to deviate is immaterial — there must be a deviation in fact to discharge the insurer." Principle: "To revisit is to deviate" — even if ship returns to original route, deviation has already occurred.
§ 50Delay: Adventure must be prosecuted with reasonable dispatch. Unreasonable delay (without lawful excuse) discharges insurer from time delay became unreasonable.
§ 51Excused Deviation or Delay: (a) Authorised by special term in policy; (b) Caused by circumstances beyond control of master and employer; (c) Reasonably necessary to comply with express/implied warranty; (d) Reasonably necessary for safety of ship or subject matter; (e) Saving human life or aiding ship in distress where human life in danger; (f) Obtaining medical/surgical aid for person on board; (g) Caused by barratrous conduct of master/crew (if barratry is a covered peril). § 51(2): When excuse ceases, ship must resume course and prosecute voyage with reasonable dispatch.

Leading Cases on Deviation

CaseFacts & Holding

Insured Perils — Standard Marine Policy & Inchmaree Clause

Standard Marine Perils — Rules for Construction of Policy (Schedule)

PERIL 1
Perils of the Seas: "Fortuitous accidents or casualties of the seas." Does NOT include ordinary action of winds and waves. Only unexpected, accidental sea casualties.
The Xantho: Ship struck known rock — held covered (fortuitous act even if rock known to exist).
PERIL 2
Fire: Whether on board or from external source. Fire causing explosion also covered.
PERIL 3
War Perils: Covered in standard policy but subject to war exclusion clauses — requiring separate war risks insurance.
PERIL 4
Pirates: "Includes passengers who mutiny and rioters who attack the ship from the shore." Broader than ordinary meaning — includes sea pirates, mutinous passengers, shore-based rioters attacking the vessel.
PERIL 5
Thieves: "Does NOT cover clandestine theft or theft committed by any one of the ship's company (crew or passengers)." Only external violent theft by outsiders. For crew theft / pilferage — specific clause (Institute TPND Clause) required.
PERIL 6
Jettison: Deliberate throwing overboard of cargo or equipment to lighten vessel in emergency. Classic general average act.
PERIL 7
Barratry: "Every wrongful act willfully committed by the master or crew to the prejudice of the owner, or as the case may be, the charterer." Examples: smuggling by master, scuttling, deviation for personal gain, theft of cargo by crew, fraudulent sale of ship by master. NOT applicable if owner orders the act.
PERIL 8
Captures & Seizures: "Arrests, etc., of kings, princes, and peoples" = political or executive acts. Does NOT include loss from riot or ordinary judicial process.
PERIL 9
"All other perils": Ejusdem generis rule — includes only perils similar in kind to those specifically mentioned in the policy.

The Inchmaree Clause — In Detail
Institute Time Clauses — Hulls, Clause 6
▾
⚖ Thomas v. Hamilton (House of Lords, 1891) — THE ORIGIN

The steamship Inchmaree suffered damage when a donkey pump check-valve became chocked with salt, preventing water from being pumped out. Damage was due to negligence of the ship's engineer. Held: Insurer NOT liable — this was not within any standard peril listed in the policy. This created a significant gap in hull coverage, leading to the Inchmaree Clause being added to Institute Time Clauses.

What the Inchmaree Clause Covers

  • Accidents in loading, discharging, or shifting cargo or fuel.
  • Explosions on board or elsewhere.
  • Breakdown of or accident to nuclear installations or reactors.
  • Contact with aircraft, helicopters, or similar objects falling therefrom.
  • Negligence of master, officers, crew, or pilots — provided the loss or damage has NOT resulted from want of due diligence by the assured, owners, or managers.
⚖ Baxendale v. Fane ("The Brilliant")

Manager negligently docked vessel for bottom cleaning, causing damage. Under the Inchmaree clause — insurer LIABLE.

⚖ "The Pacific Queen" — LIMITATION OF INCHMAREE CLAUSE

Vessel lost due to explosion and fire. Held: Explosion was due to want of due diligence by owners/managers — NOT covered. The exclusion within the Inchmaree clause itself applied — it does not cover negligence attributable to want of due diligence by the assured/owners/managers.

All Risks Policies — ICC (A) Clause

All Risks — Key Principle

Under an all risks policy, the insured must show only that a fortuitous loss has occurred during transit — need NOT identify the specific cause. The insurer must prove the loss falls within an exclusion.

Gaunt's Case (British and Foreign Marine Insurance v. Gaunt): Wool found damaged during transit. Held: Insurer liable under all risks clause — Lord Sumner: "The insured must show only a fortuitous loss during transit."
Chester's Case: Sponges damaged by dirt and water in transit. Held: Insurer liable under all risks policy.

Even under all risks, the following are ALWAYS EXCLUDED: Inherent vice of subject matter · Natural wear and tear · Wilful misconduct of assured · Delay.

Constructive Total Loss — Section 60

⚓
Constructive Total Loss — Complete Framework
§§ 60–63 Marine Insurance Act, 1963
▾

§ 60(1): Constructive total loss where subject matter reasonably abandoned because: (a) actual total loss appears unavoidable, OR (b) could not be preserved from actual total loss without expenditure exceeding its value when expenditure had been incurred.

§ 60(2)(b): For ship — cost of repairing > value of ship when repaired. § 60(2)(c): For goods — cost of repairing + forwarding > their value on arrival.

The Test: "Is it commercially reasonable to abandon?" — objective standard of the "reasonable assured."

Key Cases

⚖ Vacuum Oil Co. v. Union Insurance — PROHIBITIVE COST OF FORWARDING

10,000 tins of petroleum insured from Alexandria to Cyprus. Vessel stranded; 6,000 tins salvaged but damaged/leaking. Held: Constructive total loss — no ship would carry damaged leaking petroleum tins; cost of transportation to destination would exceed their value on arrival. § 60(2)(c) satisfied.

⚖ Rickards v. Forestal Land — GOVERNMENT-ORDERED SCUTTLING

Cargo in German vessel insured from South America to Hong Kong. War broke out; German Government ordered vessels to neutral ports, Germany, or scuttle. Vessel scuttled. Held: Constructive total loss — scuttling pursuant to governmental mandate = maritime peril. Insurer held liable.

⚖ Hall v. Hayman — WRECK VALUE NOT TO BE TAKEN INTO ACCOUNT

Cost of repairing vessel = £30,500; vessel value = £34,000. Assured added value of wreck and claimed total loss. Held: Wreck value should NOT be taken into account in estimating repair cost. Formula: cost of repairs vs. value of ship WHEN REPAIRED — not after deducting wreck value.

⚖ Panama Oriental Steamship Co. v. Wright — SEIZURE + UNLIKELY RECOVERY

Vessel under time policy seized by Vietnamese revenue authorities for un-manifested goods. Recovery unlikely. Held: Constructive total loss — assured deprived of possession and recovery unlikely. § 60(2)(a)(i) satisfied.

§§ 64–66
Partial Losses — Particular Average, Salvage & General Average

Section 64 — Particular Average Loss

§ 64(1) — Particular Average Defined

"A particular average loss is a partial loss of the subject matter insured, caused by a peril insured against, and which is not a general average loss." Accidental and fortuitous — only the insured property is damaged — no voluntary sacrifice.

§ 64(2) — Particular Charges: Expenses incurred for safety or preservation of subject matter — other than general average and salvage charges — are called particular charges. Particular charges are NOT included in particular average.

Section 65 — Salvage Charges

"Salvage charges means charges recoverable under maritime law by a salvor independently of contract." NOT included: expenses of services rendered by the assured or his agents or persons employed for hire (those are particular charges).

Lloyd's Open Form (LOF): When ship in distress, master signs LOF with potential salvors. Headed "No cure — no pay" — if salvage attempt fails, no award (though SCOPIC modifies this).

Section 66 — General Average Loss

§ 66(2) — General Average Act Defined

"There is a general average act where any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is voluntarily and reasonably made or incurred in time of peril for the purpose of preserving the property imperilled in the common adventure."

Three Essentials of a General Average Act
  • There must be a peril threatening the common adventure.
  • The sacrifice or expenditure must be voluntary and reasonable.
  • There must be something saved as a result (if everything is lost, there is nothing to contribute).

Examples of General Average Acts

ActCategoryExplanation
Jettison of cargoGeneral Average SacrificeDuring violent storm, 50 tonnes of cargo thrown overboard to lighten ship. Cargo owner can claim contribution from all parties (ship, remaining cargo, freight).
Emergency port entryGeneral Average ExpenditureShip diverted to port of refuge for emergency repairs. Expenses of entering port, repair costs, cost of re-shipping cargo = general average expenditure.
Flooding the holdGeneral Average SacrificeFire breaks out; master floods hold with seawater to extinguish. Water damages cargo in hold. Intentional damage = general average sacrifice.

York-Antwerp Rules: International rules (last revised 2016) incorporated by reference in most bills of lading and charter parties. Provide standardised method for calculating and apportioning general average contributions. Applied by the Average Adjuster — a specialist appointed by ship-owner (paid by insurer) who issues the General Average Statement.

§ 79(1)Subrogation (total loss): Where insurer pays for total loss — he thereupon becomes entitled to take over interest of assured in whatever remains of subject matter AND is subrogated to all rights and remedies of assured from time of the casualty. Glen Line Case: Insurer who paid total loss of sunk ship entitled to sale proceeds when ship later recovered — subrogation.
§ 79(2)Subrogation (partial loss): Where insurer pays for partial loss — he acquires no title to subject matter but is subrogated to rights and remedies of assured from time of casualty — insofar as insured has been indemnified.
§ 80(1)Contribution: Where assured over-insured by double insurance — each insurer bound to contribute rateably to loss in proportion to amount for which he is liable. Yorkshire Insurance v. Nisbet: Insurer's recovery limited to amount paid — not entitled to excess.
§ 81Effect of Under-Insurance: Where assured insured for less than insurable value — he is deemed to be his own insurer in respect of the uninsured balance.

Section 77 — Suing and Labouring Clause

§ 77(4): "It is the duty of the assured and his agents, in all cases, to take such measures as may be reasonable for the purpose of averting or minimising a loss." — Codification of the duty to mitigate in marine insurance.

§ 77(1): Where policy contains a suing and labouring clause — expenses properly incurred to avert or minimise loss recoverable from insurer, even though insurer has paid for total loss or subject matter warranted FPA.

Barratry — Detailed Study

From Schedule — Barratry Defined

"Barratry includes every wrongful act willfully committed by the master or crew to the prejudice of the owner, or as the case may be, the charterer."

⚖ Samuel v. Dumas — SCUTTLING WITH OWNER'S CONNIVANCE

A ship was scuttled with the owner's connivance. Water inflowed causing the ship to sink. Held: (1) Dominant proximate cause = scuttling (deliberate act), NOT perils of the sea (water inflow). (2) Since scuttling was done with owner's connivance — insurer NOT liable. Owner cannot claim for losses caused by his own connivance. Key Principle: Barratry = wrongful act by master/crew AGAINST the owner's interest. If owner orders it = not barratry against insurer.

Section 59 — Transhipment in Marine Insurance

Where, by a peril insured against, the voyage is interrupted at an intermediate port, and the master re-ships the goods on another vessel to continue the voyage — the liability of the insurer continues notwithstanding the landing or transhipment.

Transhipment vs. Deviation: If transhipment necessitated by maritime peril = NOT deviation. If master tranships without necessity or for personal convenience = may constitute deviation.

Oliverson v. Brightman: Transhipment covered by policy; insurer's liability continues through transhipment to destination.

Topic 6 · Syllabus Unit VI
Miscellaneous Insurance — Burglary, Liability & Motor Vehicle
Section 2(13B) — Statutory Definition of Miscellaneous Insurance Business

"Miscellaneous insurance business means the business of effecting contracts of insurance which is not principally or wholly of any kind or kinds included in clauses (6A), (11) and (13A)"

What This Means

Anything that is NOT Fire Insurance (§ 6A), NOT Life Insurance (§ 11), and NOT Marine Insurance (§ 13A) falls into miscellaneous insurance. This is a residuary / catch-all category. The three sub-types we examine: (1) Burglary Insurance · (2) Liability Insurance · (3) Motor Vehicle Insurance.

I
Burglary Insurance — Definition, Nature & Scope

1.1 Critical Distinctions — Burglary vs. Allied Terms

TermMeaningStatutory Reference
BurglaryBreaking into premises BY NIGHT with intent to commit a felony. Entry must be forcible and between certain hours (typically sunset to sunrise — common law definition).Common law + IPC
HousebreakingBreaking into premises BY DAY with intent to commit a felony.§ 445 IPC (§ 331 BNS 2023)
TheftDishonest removal of movable property without consent of owner.§ 378 IPC (§ 303 BNS)

Why the Distinction Matters in Insurance Law

A policy covering "burglary" may NOT cover "theft" committed by an employee, because there is no breaking and entering. Classic example: if a watchman steals goods = theft, NOT burglary. If policy only covers burglary → claim FAILS.

Crippen v. Hartford Fire Insurance: Burglary requires actual breaking and entry — merely finding the door open and walking in does NOT constitute burglary for insurance purposes.

1.2 Nature of Burglary Insurance

CharacteristicContent
Contract of IndemnityLike all general insurance — pure indemnity. Insured recovers only actual loss. No profit.
Uberrima FidesMust disclose: previous burglaries, security arrangements, nature of goods stored, whether premises left unattended at night. Non-disclosure vitiates the policy.
Insurable InterestMust have insurable interest at time of loss. Owners, mortgagees, bailees, warehouse keepers — all have insurable interest.
Proximate CauseCausa proxima applies. If goods lost due to fire during a burglary — proximate cause determination becomes critical for which policy responds.
Personal ContractLike fire insurance — if property sold/transferred, policy does NOT automatically pass to new owner.
Connivance of InsuredIf insured connives in or stages the burglary — policy is void. Principle: ex turpi causa non oritur actio — no man shall benefit from his own wrong.

1.3 Scope — Covered & Excluded

Covered
Loss of / damage to insured property caused by burglary or housebreaking · Damage to premises during forcible entry or exit · Loss by robbery (if specifically included) · Personal accident to insured during attempted robbery (some policies)
Standard Exclusions
Theft without breaking (e.g., employee theft without forced entry) · Fire during burglary (covered under fire policy) · Mysterious disappearance (no evidence of criminal act) · War and civil commotion · Acts of insured · Domestic servant theft (unless specifically included) · Goods not on premises at time of loss

Types of Burglary Policies

Key Definitions

Section 3 — No-Fault Liability — The Most Important Provision

Section 3(1) — Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991

"Where death or injury to any person (other than a workman) or damage to any property has resulted from an accident, the owner shall be liable to give such relief as is specified in the Schedule."

Section 3(2) — No Proof of Negligence Required

"The claimant shall NOT be required to plead and establish that the death, injury or damage in respect of which the claim has been made was due to any wrongful act, neglect or default of any person."

This is a statutory departure from the common law principle that liability requires proof of fault. It is strict liability — modelled on the principle from Rylands v. Fletcher (1868) LR 3 HL 330: "A person who brings onto his land something likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it at his peril. If he fails to do so, he is prima facie answerable for all the damage which is the natural consequence of its escape."

Section 4 — Mandatory Insurance

"Every owner shall take out, before he starts handling any hazardous substance, one or more insurance policies..." Failure is a penal offence under Section 14 (imprisonment not less than 1 year 6 months but may extend to 6 years, or fine not less than ₹1 lakh, or both).

Section 7 — Award of Relief & Schedule — Quantum

Critical Note — Schedule Amounts are MINIMUM Immediate Relief

These are immediate relief amounts — NOT full compensation. The victim retains the right under § 8(1) to pursue higher compensation under any other law (tort law, CPC, etc.). Under § 8(2), the relief already paid under this Act is deducted from any such higher compensation.

Section 7A — Environmental Relief Fund

Central Government may, by notification, establish an Environmental Relief Fund for paying relief under awards. Ensures victims are not left remediless even if the insurer is insolvent.

III
Motor Vehicle Insurance — Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 (§§ 140–176)
Most Important Preliminary Note

Motor vehicle insurance is the most practically significant form of miscellaneous insurance in India — both by premium volume and by litigation volume. The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 (replaced the MV Act, 1939) was comprehensively amended by the Motor Vehicles (Amendment) Act, 2019 — which significantly increased compensation amounts and introduced new reforms.

Section 140 — No-Fault Liability (Absolute Liability)

Section 140(1) — Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

"Where death or permanent disablement of any person has resulted from an accident arising out of the use of a motor vehicle or motor vehicles, the owner of the vehicle shall, or, as the case may be, the owners of the vehicles shall, jointly and severally, be liable to pay compensation in respect of such death or disablement."

Sub-sectionContent
§ 140(2)Fixed compensation: Death = ₹50,000 · Permanent Disablement = ₹25,000. [Pre-2019 Amendment statutory amounts. Post-2019 Amendment, MACT awards much higher amounts under § 166 based on structured formula.]
§ 140(3) ★"The claimant shall NOT be required to plead and establish that the death or permanent disablement was due to any wrongful act, neglect or default." Essence of No-Fault Liability — absolute liability without proof of negligence.
§ 140(4)A claim under § 140 shall NOT be defeated by reason of any contributory negligence of the deceased/disabled person.
§ 140(5)No-fault compensation is in addition to any other compensation payable under any other provision.
⚖ MC Mehta v. Union of India, AIR 1987 SC 1086 (Oleum Gas Leak Case)

Justice P.N. Bhagwati held that enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities are liable absolutely for any harm resulting therefrom — they cannot escape liability even by proving they were not negligent. This goes BEYOND Rylands v. Fletcher (which had exceptions for Act of God and consent of plaintiff). This is the Indian constitutional basis of absolute liability, operating alongside § 140 of MV Act.

Section 146 — Compulsory Third-Party Insurance — The Foundation

Section 146(1) — Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

"No person shall use, except as a passenger, or cause or allow any other person to use, a motor vehicle in a public place unless there is in force in relation to the use of the vehicle by that person or that other person, as the case may be, a policy of insurance complying with the requirements of this Chapter."

Nature
Compulsory — not optional
Coverage Area
"In a public place"
Both owner AND permitted persons must be covered
Policy must comply with Chapter XI requirements
Punishment for Violation (§ 196)
Imprisonment up to 3 months, or fine up to ₹2,000, or both (2019 Amendment: fine = ₹2,000)

Section 147 — Requirements of Policies & Limits of Liability

SectionContent
§ 147(1)A policy complies with Chapter XI requirements only if: (a) issued by authorized insurer (authorized under Insurance Act, 1938); (b) insures the person against any liability which may be incurred in respect of — death of or bodily injury to any person (including owner of goods or authorized representative being carried) + damage to property of a third party.
§ 147(2)Policy may exclude liability: (a) while vehicle used outside specified geographical area; (b) when driven by unlicensed driver; (c) when driven by person disqualified from holding licence.
§ 147(5)Policy covering death/bodily injury need NOT fix any limit on amount of liability — UNLIMITED in respect of persons.
§ 147(6)In respect of damage to third-party property — liability limited to ₹7,50,000 (subject to amendment).

Section 149 — Duty of Insurers to Satisfy Judgments — "Pay and Recover" Principle

Section 149(1) — Motor Vehicles Act, 1988

"If, after a certificate of insurance has been issued, any liability is incurred which is covered by the policy, then the insurer shall, notwithstanding that the insurer may be entitled to avoid or cancel the policy, pay to the person entitled to the benefit of a decree, any sum not exceeding the sum insured in respect of that liability."

What this means: Even if the insurer could legitimately avoid the policy (insured violated a condition) — the insurer must FIRST pay the third party and THEN recover from the insured. The third party's rights are NOT defeated by the insured's misconduct.

§ 149(2) — Limited Defences: Insurer can avoid liability to third party only on: (a) breach of condition relating to age, disqualification, or competency of driver; (b) use of vehicle for purposes not permitted by policy.

⚖ National Insurance Co. v. Swaran Singh, (2004) 3 SCC 297 — LANDMARK CASE

Supreme Court distinguished between: (1) Fundamental breach (e.g., vehicle used as taxi under private policy) — insurer CAN avoid; (2) Technical breach (e.g., minor licence irregularity) — insurer MUST pay third party and recover from insured. This framework governs § 149 disputes.

⚖ Oriental Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Nanjappan, (2004) 13 SCC 224

Supreme Court confirmed that the insurer must satisfy a third-party decree even when there is a breach of policy condition (e.g., unlicensed driver) — and then recover from the insured.

Section 157 — Transfer of Certificate of Insurance

When a vehicle is transferred, the certificate of insurance and the policy shall be deemed to have been transferred in favour of the transferee. This automatically protects the new owner and third parties.

⚖ Rikhi Ram v. Sukhrania, AIR 2003 SC 2930

Supreme Court held that upon transfer of vehicle, the insurance policy is automatically transferred and the insurer continues to be liable for third-party claims even after transfer.

Sections 163A & 163B — Structured Formula for Compensation

Section 163A: Provides for structured formula for compensation on account of death or permanent disablement — based on table in the Second Schedule. Considers: annual income of victim, age of victim, nature and extent of disability. Introduced to ensure uniformity and reduce litigation over quantum.

Sections 165–176 — Motor Accidents Claims Tribunals (MACT)

SectionContent
§ 165Constitution of MACT: Every State Government shall constitute one or more Motor Accidents Claims Tribunals for adjudicating compensation claims.
§ 175Exclusive jurisdiction: No civil court has jurisdiction to entertain claims that fall within MACT's purview.
§ 166Application for Compensation: Any person claiming compensation may apply to the Claims Tribunal. Time limit: within 6 months of the accident (tribunal can condone delay for sufficient cause).
§ 168Award: Tribunal makes an award specifying amount of compensation payable and person by whom payable.
§ 169Tribunal Powers: Claims Tribunal has all powers of a civil court under CPC.
§ 173Appeals: Appeal from MACT award lies to the High Court. No appeal from award for amount less than ₹10,000.

Hit-and-Run — Solatium Scheme (Section 161)

For victims of hit-and-run accidents where the offending vehicle cannot be traced. Post-2019 Amendment amounts: Death = ₹2,00,000 (increased from ₹25,000) · Grievous Injury = ₹50,000 (increased from ₹12,500). The Solatium Fund is maintained by the General Insurance Council.
