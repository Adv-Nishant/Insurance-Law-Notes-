Life Insurance — A Comprehensive Lecture
I
Definition of Life Insurance
Foundational Principle

Life insurance occupies a unique position in the entire law of insurance. Unlike fire or marine insurance where the subject matter is tangible property capable of monetary valuation, here the subject matter is human life — incapable of precise pecuniary estimation. This single distinction cascades into almost every principle of the topic.

Statutory Definition — Section 2(11), Insurance Act, 1938

"The business of effecting contracts of insurance upon human life, including any contract whereby the payment of money is assured on death (except death by accident only) or the happening of any contingency dependent on human life, and any contract which is subject to payment of premiums for a term dependent on human life."

Section 2(11) — Deemed to Include

#CategoryDetail
1Disability & Accident BenefitsGranting of disability and double or triple indemnity accident benefits, if so provided in the contract.
2AnnuitiesGranting of annuities upon human life.
3SuperannuationGranting of superannuation allowances and benefits payable out of any fund for persons in a particular profession, trade, or employment, or their dependants.
4ULIPs 2010 AmendmentExplanation inserted by Act 26 of 2010: "life insurance business shall include any unit linked insurance policy (ULIP) or scrips or any such instrument which provides a component of investment and a component of insurance."
Hardy Ivamy — Doctrinal Definition

"A contract whereby one person called the 'insurer' undertakes in return for the agreed consideration called the 'premium' to pay to another person called the 'insured' a sum of money or its equivalent on the happening of a specified event."

The Landmark Case — Dalby v. India & London Life Assurance Co. (1854)

⚖ Dalby v. India & London Life Assurance Co. (1854) — HOUSE OF LORDS — MUST KNOW

Facts: John Wright took out four policies of £3,000 each on the life of the Duke of Cambridge with "A" Insurance Co. (subsidiary of G). He later took re-insurance for £1,000 with the principal company G. The original four policies were surrendered. When the Duke died, G contended: no insurable interest at time of loss since original policies surrendered.

Held: House of Lords rejected G's contention and laid down the fundamental rule: "Life insurance is NOT a contract of indemnity."

Rule: Insurable interest need exist only at the time of entering the contract, NOT at the time of the loss. This is the cardinal distinction from fire and marine insurance.

II
Essentials of Life Insurance

General Essentials (Indian Contract Act, 1872)

  • Offer & Acceptance: Proposal form = offer; insurer's acceptance after medical report + first premium + agent's report = acceptance. Policy document = evidence of contract.
  • Free Consent: Free from coercion (§ 15), undue influence (§ 16), fraud (§ 17), misrepresentation (§ 18), mistake (§ 20–22).
  • Lawful Consideration: Premium = insured's consideration; insurer's promise to pay = insurer's consideration.
  • Capacity of Parties: Both must satisfy § 11 Contract Act — majority (18 yrs), sound mind, not disqualified. A minor cannot directly enter, but guardian can take a policy for minor's benefit.
  • Lawful Object: Purpose must be legal. Policy with fraudulent intent is void.

Specific Essentials of Life Insurance

EssentialRule / Key Point
Insurable InterestWithout it = void, indistinguishable from wagering (§ 30 Contract Act). Must exist at time of effecting policy only (Dalby). No requirement at time of claim.
Uberrima FidesHighest standard of good faith. LIC v. Shakuntala Devi — trivial ailments need not be disclosed. Godfrey v. Brittanic — serious conditions must be disclosed.
Insurable RiskThe event (death / survival) must be a genuine risk. Death is certain; its time is uncertain — satisfying the uncertainty requirement.
Premium§ 64VB: No risk assumed unless premium received in advance. Days of grace typically 30 days; if death occurs during grace period — claim paid, unpaid premium deducted.
Policy DocumentEvidence of the contract. Contains all terms, sum assured, premium schedule, exclusions.
III
Nature of Life Insurance — Six Defining Characteristics
CharacteristicExplanation
1. NOT a Contract of Indemnity Definitively established in Dalby. Life is incapable of monetary valuation. Insured (or beneficiary) receives full sum assured regardless of actual loss. Consequences: No concept of over-insurance in life insurance — all multiple policies pay; subrogation does NOT apply; contribution does NOT apply between life insurers.
2. Aleatory Contract Speculative / contingent — insurer's obligation depends on a future uncertain event (death or survival to a specified date). Unlike a wager, backed by insurable interest and spirit of protection.
3. Contract of Assurance Death is certain — only the time is uncertain. The insurer is not "insuring" against a possible event but "assuring" payment on the certainty of that event. Hence called "assurance," not "insurance."
4. Unilateral After Formation Once formed, insurer's obligations are primary and absolute (subject to conditions). Insured need not continue paying premiums — can let policy lapse — but insurer cannot cancel arbitrarily.
5. Investment + Protection Unique dual character: Protection (against premature death) + Investment/Savings (premiums accumulate, policy acquires surrender value, bonuses declared). Contrasts with fire/marine which are purely protective.
6. Long-Term Contract Spans entire decades or whole life. Contrast: fire/marine — typically annual contracts.
V
Events Insured Against — Including Suicide

A. Primary Events Triggering Liability

EventCoverage Rule
Death of InsuredPrimary event. Covers: natural death, accidental death, death by third-party act (murder), death during surgery. Standard rule: ALL causes of death covered unless specifically excluded.
Survival to Specified DateIn endowment policies — sum assured payable at end of term if insured is alive.
Attaining Specified AgePension/retirement trigger — policy stipulates a particular age upon reaching which sum assured becomes payable.

B. Events NOT Covered — Important Exceptions

⚖ Cleaver v. Mutual Reserve — MURDER BY BENEFICIARY

M insured his life in favour of wife W. W poisoned M and was convicted. Held: W could NOT recover — "no person shall profit from their own wrong" (ex turpi causa non oritur actio). However, executors of M's estate could recover and hold for M's creditors and heirs. Principle: implied term theory — prevents unjust enrichment from wrongful acts.

⚖ Liberty National Life Insurance Co. v. Weldon

Nurse took life policy on her 2-year-old niece. She administered arsenic, child died. Court imposed damages on the insurance company for issuing policy without verifying insurable interest. Nurse could not claim.

C. Suicide — The Most Debated Event in Life Insurance Law

⚖
English Law vs. Indian Law on Suicide
▾
DimensionEnglish LawIndian Law
Status of Suicide Historically a crime (felo de se — felon of himself). Decriminalized by Suicide Act, 1961. Not a criminal offence. Attempt to commit suicide was criminalized under § 309 IPC but largely decriminalized by Mental Healthcare Act, 2017.
Leading Case Beresford v. Royal Insurance Co. (1938) — Major R shot himself (9 years after policy, past the 1-year exclusion). Lord Atkin: suicide was a crime; contractual obligation from crime cannot be enforced. Held: Claim REJECTED. Kankaya Lal's Case — Policy stated void if suicide within one year. K, after 13 months, took poison suspecting wife's fidelity. Held: Suicide AFTER one-year exclusion period — claim UPHELD.
General Position English rule from Beresford does NOT apply in India in same manner (suicide not a crime here). Life insurance claim for death by suicide is generally ALLOWED in India, subject to specific exclusion clauses in the policy.

The Standard Suicide Exclusion Clause (LIC Policies)

"If the life assured commits suicide within one year from the date of commencement of the policy (or from the date of revival), the policy shall be void and the insurer shall not be liable to pay the claim."

Sane vs. Insane Suicide

⚖ Borradaile v. Hunter

Policy stated: "If the assured should die by his or her own hand, or by the hands of justice, or in consequence of a duel, the policy shall be void." A man threw himself into a river and drowned. Held: Act of self-destruction — policy void under the clause. Courts generally do not distinguish between sane and insane suicide under a general "death by own hand" clause.

Rule: If exclusion is "death by own hand" → applies whether sane or insane. If exclusion is "intentional self-destruction" → insanity at time of suicide may be a defence (no intention if insane).

Section 45 & Suicide — Critical Link

§ 45 three-year protection applies equally to suicide claims. If life assured commits suicide after three years of the policy being in force, the insurer cannot use any misstatement in the proposal as a ground to avoid the claim.

VII
Circumstances Affecting the Risk in Life Insurance
1. Age
Single most important factor. Premium rates directly correlated with age. Misstatement of age → sum assured adjusted (not void), unlike misstatement of health.
2. Health & Physical Condition
Most closely scrutinised. Chronic diseases, past illnesses, hospitalisations, disabilities must be disclosed. LIC v. Shakuntala Devi — trivial ailments (indigestion, headache) need not be disclosed.
3. Occupation
Low-risk (desk jobs, teachers) → standard rates. High-risk (miners, pilots, explosives workers) → "occupational loading" (extra premium). Change to more hazardous occupation must be informed.
4. Habits of Life (Moral Hazard)
Smoking → typically 20–25% extra premium. Alcoholism, drug use, dangerous hobbies (skydiving, mountaineering) → material facts. Criminal background → higher moral hazard.
5. Family History
Hereditary diseases (heart disease, cancer, diabetes, mental illness). Longevity of parents and siblings. Extra premium or proposal decline if bad hereditary history.
6. Geographical Position / Residence
Endemic zones (malaria, dengue), high-altitude areas, conflict zones. Extra premium or exclusion clause for persons residing in / travelling to conflict zones.
7. Financial Position
Capacity to pay premiums sustainably. Insurer ensures sum assured is not disproportionate to income (moral hazard check — temptation to cause death for financial gain).
8. Existing Insurance
Number and value of existing policies must be disclosed. No legal bar on multiple policies, but insurer needs to know total insurance in force to assess moral hazard.
VIII
Categories / Kinds of Life Insurance Policies

Classification I — By Duration / Term

Duration-Based
1. Whole Life Policy
Sum assured payable ONLY on death — whenever it occurs. Runs for the entire lifetime. No maturity benefit, only death benefit. Policy accumulates cash value over time. Premium may be paid throughout life (ordinary) or for a limited period (limited payment whole life).
Duration-Based
2. Term Insurance Policy
Purest form — protection only, no investment. If insured dies during term → sum assured paid. If insured survives term → NOTHING paid, policy lapses. Lowest premium. No maturity benefit, no surrender value. Ideal for young breadwinners with dependants.
Convertible Term — option to convert to endowment/whole life without fresh medical exam.
Duration-Based · Most Popular in India
3. Endowment Policy
Combines insurance + savings. Sum assured payable on EITHER death during term OR survival to end of term (maturity). Dual benefit. Policy acquires surrender value after 3 years. Bonuses added (participating policies).
Double Endowment — pays double sum assured on maturity; single on death.
Duration-Based · Endowment Variant
4. Money Back Policy
Periodic survival benefits paid during policy term (e.g., 20% of sum assured every 5 years). If death occurs at any time → FULL sum assured paid (periodic payments already made are NOT deducted). Provides liquidity during the policy term.

Topic 4 · Syllabus Unit IV
Fire Insurance — A Comprehensive Lecture
Critical Preliminary Note

Fire insurance is classified under "general insurance business" (§ 2(6B), Insurance Act, 1938). Unlike life insurance (governed by LIC Act) or marine insurance (governed by MIA 1963), fire insurance in India has NO dedicated statutory code. It is governed by common law principles, judicial decisions, the Standard Fire Policy approved by the Tariff Advisory Committee, and IRDAI guidelines. This makes case law exceptionally important in fire insurance.

I
Definition of Fire Insurance
Statutory Definition — Section 2(6A), Insurance Act, 1938

"The business of effecting, otherwise than incidentally to some other class of insurance business, contracts of insurance against loss by or incidental to fire or other occurrence customarily included among the risks insured against in fire insurance policies."

Element 1
Must be the PRIMARY business, not incidental to another class
Element 2
Coverage is against loss BY or INCIDENTAL to fire
Element 3
Extends to other occurrences CUSTOMARILY included in fire policies
Cotton LJ in Castellain v. Preston (1883) — Most Quoted Judicial Definition

"A contract whereby one person undertakes in return for agreed consideration to indemnify another person against loss or damage occasioned by fire up to the agreed amount."

II
Nature of Fire Insurance
CharacteristicKey Content
1. Contract of Indemnity Fundamental characteristic. Insured placed in same financial position after loss — no better, no worse. No profit from insurance. Castellain v. Preston: "The contract of insurance is a contract of indemnity, and of indemnity only." [Also leading case on subrogation.]
2. Personal Contract Policy attached to the person, not the property. If insured sells property — policy does NOT automatically transfer. New owner must take fresh policy. Exception: With insurer's written consent, policy may be assigned/transferred with the property. Rayner v. Preston: policy personal to seller; buyer could not claim without assignment.
3. Uberrima Fides Material facts: nature and use of property, construction type, location, previous fire claims, existence of other insurance, moral hazard. Halves v. RFA: fire crackers brought to shop — not disclosed — insurer not liable. Dawson's Case: misrepresented building construction — insurer not liable.
4. Indivisible Contract Generally covers the entire insured property (building, contents, stock, machinery) as one indivisible whole. Individual items may be separately scheduled within a single policy.
5. Short-Term Contract Typically an annual contract (one year). Renewed each year. Standard clause: "The policy expires at 4 P.M. on the last day noted in the policy."
6. Strict Compliance with Conditions Policies contain conditions precedent to liability — prompt notice, preservation of damaged property, submission of claim within stipulated time, cooperation with surveyor. Non-compliance may defeat a claim entirely.

IV
Meaning of "Fire" — The Most Debated Topic in Fire Insurance
Critical Note

The Insurance Act, 1938 and the Standard Fire Policy do NOT define "fire." Courts have developed the meaning through case law. This is one of the most frequently examined topics — know the cases with precision.

Three Essential Elements for a Valid Fire Insurance Claim

  • Actual Ignition: Mere generation of heat without ignition does NOT constitute fire. Combustion with a flame or glow is essential.
  • The Fire Must Be HOSTILE, Not FRIENDLY — most important judicial distinction in fire insurance law.
  • The Fire Must Not Be Caused by Wilful Act of the Insured — arson by insured = ground for avoiding the policy entirely.
🔥
Friendly Fire vs. Hostile Fire — Key Distinction
▾
TypeDefinitionCoverage
Friendly FireFire confined to its proper place, burning for its intended purpose — fire in a furnace, cooking fire, heating grate.Generally NOT covered (damage from intentional burning is not accidental).
Hostile FireFire that has escaped its proper boundaries and become uncontrolled.COVERED.
⚖ Harris v. Poland — FRIENDLY FIRE CAUSING ACCIDENTAL LOSS = COVERED

Mrs. Harris concealed jewellery and currency notes under the grate (fireplace), placed coal and wood over them, then went out. On returning, she forgot about the concealed items and inadvertently lit the grate. By the time she remembered, jewellery was damaged and notes were burnt.

Held: The insurer WAS LIABLE. Though the fire originated as a friendly fire (in the grate for heating), it caused loss to property not intended to be burnt. The items were not deliberately placed there to be burned — their burning was accidental and unintended.

Principle: If property NOT intended to be burnt is destroyed by fire — even a "friendly fire" — the loss IS covered under the fire policy.

⚖ Thompson's Case — STRICT CONSTRUCTION OF EXCLUSION CLAUSES

Fire policy excepted liability if gasoline was "stored" in the drugstore. The owner was using a gasoline stove for routine heating purposes. Fire broke out.

Held: Insurer WAS LIABLE. Using a stove for routine purposes did NOT constitute "storing" gasoline. Exclusion clause read narrowly against insurer.

Principle: Contra proferentem — ambiguities and exclusions in insurance policies are construed narrowly against the insurer.

Coverage — Loss Incidental to Fire (What IS Included)

Fire Brigade Damage
Water damage from hoses, damage from axes breaking open doors — all covered.
Smoke Damage
Covered as incidental to fire.
Chemical Suppressants
If foam/chemical suppressants damage goods — covered.
Necessary Demolition
Building demolished to prevent fire from spreading — demolition loss covered.
Removal of Goods
Goods damaged while being removed from burning premises to safety — may be covered.
⚖ Symington v. Union Insurance of Canton — PREVENTIVE DAMAGE COVERED

Cork stored in warehouse insured against fire. Fire broke out some distance away. To prevent fire from spreading, some quantity of cork was thrown into the sea. Held: Insurer liable for BOTH (1) damage caused while putting out fire AND (2) cork destroyed by throwing into sea. Both losses = proximate cause = fire peril.

VII
Average Clause — Under-Insurance Penalty
Average Clause — Condition 10, Standard Fire Policy

Operates when the insured has taken insurance for less than the actual value of the property (under-insurance). The insured is treated as a co-insurer for the uninsured portion and must bear the loss proportionately.

Average Clause Formula
Amount Payable = (Sum Insured ÷ Actual Value of Property) × Amount of Loss
Illustration: Umesh insures property for ₹1,50,000 when actual value = ₹2,50,000. Fire loss = ₹90,000.
Amount Payable = (1,50,000 ÷ 2,50,000) × 90,000 = ₹54,000
Balance ₹36,000 borne by Umesh — he becomes his own insurer for the under-insured portion.

Exception: If sum insured is at least 85% of actual value → average clause does NOT apply.

Why does the Average Clause exist? To prevent deliberate under-insurance. Without it, insured persons would under-insure to pay lower premiums and yet recover the full (typically partial) loss. The average clause removes this incentive and penalizes under-insurance.

VIII
Categories of Fire Insurance Policies
Fire Policy Type
1. Valued Policy
Parties agree on the value of subject matter at time of contract. Agreed value stated in policy. On total/partial loss — agreed value is the measure (no dispute about actual loss). Common for antiques, art, items of uncertain market value. In absence of fraud — agreed value is conclusive.
Fire Policy Type · Most Common
2. Unvalued Policy (Open Policy)
Actual value NOT specified — only maximum limit (sum insured). Actual loss must be proved at time of claim. Insurer pays actual loss proved (subject to sum insured). Average clause applies if under-insurance present.
Fire Policy Type
3. Specific Policy
Covers specific item(s) listed in policy for a specific amount. Sum insured = maximum liability. If less than actual value → average clause applies. Distinguished from valued policy: stated amount = maximum limit, not agreed indemnity.
Fire Policy Type · Widest Cover
4. Comprehensive Policy
Widest coverage — fire, lightning, explosion, aircraft, riots, strike, storm, flood, earthquake, burglary, and more — all under one policy. Also called "all risks" policy (though exclusions still exist). Ideal for businesses wanting broad protection.
Fire Policy Type · Multiple Locations
5. Floating Policy
Covers property at different locations under a single policy with a single sum insured. Each location and value of goods must be disclosed. Ideal for traders/manufacturers with goods at multiple godowns/warehouses. Total value across all locations should not exceed sum insured.
Fire Policy Type · Fluctuating Stock
6. Declaration Policy
Ideal for traders whose stock values fluctuate during the year. A provisional sum insured is fixed. Premium paid on this provisional sum. Each month → insured declares highest value of stock held during that month. Year-end average = actual sum insured. If actual average < provisional sum → premium refund (up to 50% of difference).
Fire Policy Type · Combination
7. Floater Declaration Policy
Combination of floating + declaration policies. Covers fluctuating stock at multiple locations with monthly declarations of value at each location.
Fire Policy Type · No Depreciation
8. Replacement / Reinstatement Value Policy
Insurer pays cost of replacing/reinstating damaged property to condition "as new" — WITHOUT deduction for depreciation. Normal policy: market value = replacement cost minus depreciation. This policy: no depreciation deducted — new-for-old replacement. Usually for plant, machinery, buildings.
Fire Policy Type · Supplementary
9. Consequential Loss Policy
Covers INDIRECT losses from fire that standard fire policy would NOT cover. Covers: loss of net profit during business closure, standing charges (rent, interest, salaries continuing even when business closed), increased cost of working, auditors' fees. Must be taken ALONG WITH a standard fire policy — cannot stand alone. Indemnity period: typically 6–12 months.
IX
Special Doctrines in Fire Insurance — Reinstatement, Subrogation, Contribution
🏗
Doctrine 1: Reinstatement
Clause 7 — Standard Fire Policy
▾

Meaning: Reinstatement is the insurer's option (or insured's entitlement under specific clauses) to restore the damaged property to its original condition as an alternative to making monetary payment.

Clause 7 — Standard Fire Policy (Tariff Advisory Committee)

"The insurer has the option to reinstate or replace the property damaged or destroyed, instead of paying the amount of loss or damage. The maximum shall not be more than the insured sum."

AspectRule
Insurer's ElectionOnce insurer elects to reinstate — they are BOUND to complete it. If they elect but fail to complete properly → liable for damages for the failure.
Insured's RightWithout a specific "Reinstatement Value Clause" in the policy — insured CANNOT demand reinstatement. The option belongs to the insurer, not the insured.
ScopeMust reinstate to condition substantially equivalent to pre-fire condition. If reinstatement impossible (e.g., unique historical structure) → insurer must pay monetary value.
LimitationMust reinstate at the SAME property at the SAME location — cannot use proceeds to build at a different site. Maximum = total sum assured.
Indemnity TensionStrict indemnity = market value (replacement cost minus depreciation). Reinstatement = new-for-old — potentially more than actual financial loss. Resolution: When reinstatement clause is in policy, principle of indemnity is modified by agreement — reinstatement value supersedes strict market value.

Doctrine 2: Subrogation
§§ 79–80 Marine Insurance Act, 1963 (by analogy) · Common Law Equitable Doctrine for Fire Insurance
▾

Definition: Subrogation is the right of the insurer, upon payment of the claim, to step into the shoes of the insured and exercise all rights, remedies, and causes of action that the insured had against third parties responsible for the loss.

"Every day the insurer is put in the place of the insured." — Lord Mansfield in Burnand v. Rodocanachi — The courts: "The assured cannot take from both hands."
⚖ Castellain v. Preston (1883) — THE LEADING SUBROGATION CASE — MUST KNOW PERFECTLY

Facts: Preston contracted to sell his house to a purchaser (purchaser's obligation to pay = unconditional). Before sale was completed, fire destroyed the house. Preston claimed from fire insurer and received the full loss amount (£330). The purchase was later completed and purchaser paid the full (pre-fire) purchase price. Preston received DOUBLE recovery — insurance claim + full purchase price.

Held (Brett LJ): Insurance company entitled to recover the full amount it had paid (£330) from Preston. Preston had not actually suffered a loss — the purchaser paid the full value. Insurer was subrogated to Preston's right to receive the purchase consideration.

Principle: "The contract of insurance is a contract of indemnity, and of indemnity only... the insurer can only be called upon to make good that loss which the insured has actually, not fictitiously, sustained."

Mechanics of Subrogation

  • Loss occurs due to fire caused/contributed to by a third party.
  • Insurer pays the claim to the insured.
  • Insurer now subrogated — acquires insured's rights against the third party (in tort/contract/statute/gifts received).
  • Insurer sues the third party in the insured's name (not insurer's own name — because insured's right runs against the third party, not insurer's).
  • Whatever insurer recovers: up to amount of claim paid = belongs to insurer; any excess = belongs to insured.
⚖ Yorkshire Insurance Co. v. Nisbet Shipping Co. — LIMIT OF SUBROGATION

Canadian Government vessel caused total loss to Vessel A. Insurer paid £72,000. Owners then sued Canadian Government and recovered £127,000 (due to currency fluctuations). Insurer sued owners to recover. Held: Insurer entitled to £72,000 only — exactly what they paid. Cannot profit from subrogation.

Key Principles of Subrogation

PrincipleRule
Arises after full paymentSubrogation right arises only upon actual payment of the total loss. Partial payment = proportionate subrogation rights.
Limited to amount paidInsurer's claim is limited to what they paid — they cannot profit from subrogation.
Insured must not compromiseInsured must not compromise or settle rights against third party without insurer's consent — if they do, they may be liable to the insurer.
NOT for life insuranceSubrogation does NOT apply to life insurance — because life insurance is not a contract of indemnity.
Sue in insured's nameInsurer cannot sue a third party in their own name — must sue in the insured's name.
Can be excludedSubrogation rights can be expressly excluded by the policy terms.

Doctrine 3: Contribution
Double Insurance · § 34 Marine Insurance Act, 1963 (by analogy)
▾

Definition: Contribution is the right of one insurer who has paid a claim to compel other insurers also liable for the same loss on the same subject matter to share the payment proportionately.

Conditions for Double Insurance (when contribution arises)

  • Two or more policies.
  • Covering the same subject matter.
  • Against the same peril.
  • In favour of the same insured.
  • Total sum insured exceeds actual value of insured property.
Contribution Formula
Contribution of Each Insurer = (Individual Policy Amount ÷ Total of All Policy Amounts) × Actual Loss
Illustration — Smt. Rani's property (actual value ₹80,000) insured with A for ₹30,000 and B for ₹20,000. Total sum insured = ₹50,000. Fire loss = ₹25,000.

A's share = (30,000 ÷ 50,000) × 25,000 = ₹15,000
B's share = (20,000 ÷ 50,000) × 25,000 = ₹10,000

Smt. Rani can (i) claim ₹15,000 from A and ₹10,000 from B directly, OR (ii) claim full ₹25,000 from A — A then recovers ₹10,000 from B by way of contribution.
⚖ North British and Mercantile Insurance Co. v. Liverpool London and Globe Insurance Co.

Two fire insurance companies had each insured the same warehouse. A fire occurred. Held: Both companies liable to contribute proportionately to the loss. Neither could avoid contribution by arguing the other policy was "primary."

Doctrine 4: Proximate Cause — Applied to Fire Insurance
"Causa proxima non remota spectatur"
▾

Rules for Applying Proximate Cause in Fire Insurance

RuleCase & Holding
Rule 1: Single cause — covered fire perilInsurer liable. Single cause — excepted peril → insurer not liable.
Rule 2: Multiple simultaneous causesCourt identifies dominant, proximate cause and decides liability accordingly.
Rule 3: Chain of causation — excluded peril triggers fireTootal v. London Fire Insurance Co.: Earthquake (excluded) caused fire; wind spread it to T's building. Held: Proximate cause = EARTHQUAKE, not fire. Insurer NOT liable.
Rule 4: Novus actus interveniensMarsden v. City Insurance: Fire occurred → mob plundered → plate-glass broken. Held: Proximate cause of glass breakage = mob's lawlessness (independent cause), NOT fire. Insurer NOT liable for glass.
Rule 5: Acts to prevent greater loss — coveredSymington v. Union Insurance: Cork thrown into sea to prevent fire spreading. Held: COVERED — preventive damage proximately caused by fire peril.
Rule 6: Burden of proofInsured proves covered fire as cause. If insurer relies on exception — insurer bears the burden of proving exception applies. Thompson's Case: Insurer failed to prove "storing" gasoline applied to routine stove use.

Rights & Duties of Parties in Fire Insurance

Duties of the Insured

PhaseDuty
Before the ContractDisclose all material facts (utmost good faith). Answer all proposal form questions truthfully. Not misrepresent nature, value, or condition of property.
During Currency of PolicyNot increase the risk without informing insurer. Pay premiums on time. Inform insurer of any other insurance on same property. Maintain the property reasonably.
After the LossGive immediate notice of fire to insurer (condition precedent). Notify fire brigade and police (suspected arson → FIR). Take steps to minimise loss (duty to mitigate). Protect salvageable property. NOT remove or alter damaged property before surveyor assesses it. Submit claim in prescribed form within stipulated time. Provide all documentation. Cooperate with surveyor.

Rights of the Insurer

  • Right to Inspect Premises — both before issuing policy and after fire.
  • Right to Take Possession After Fire — enter premises, take possession, take measures to protect damaged property.
  • Right to Sell Salvage — arrange sale of salvaged goods; credit salvage value in claim settlement.
  • Right of Subrogation — upon payment, step into insured's shoes to sue responsible third parties.
  • Right of Contribution — seek proportionate contribution from other insurers who are also liable.
  • Right to Avoid the Policy — for non-disclosure, misrepresentation, fraud, or breach of conditions.
  • Right to Appoint Surveyor / Loss Assessor — § 64UM Insurance Act, 1938 — for claims above specified threshold, licensed surveyor must assess. Note: Surveyor's assessment is NOT binding on insurer; insurer may pay a different amount (proviso to § 64UM(4)).
