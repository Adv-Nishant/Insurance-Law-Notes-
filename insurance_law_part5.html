Topic 7 · Part I
Double Insurance — Comprehensive Lecture
§ 34
Definition — Section 34, Marine Insurance Act, 1963
Section 34(1) — Marine Insurance Act, 1963 — Statutory Definition

"Where two or more policies are effected by or on behalf of the assured on the same adventure and interest or any part thereof, and the sums insured exceed the indemnity allowed by this Act, the assured is said to be over-insured by double insurance."

Though this definition appears in the Marine Insurance Act, the principle is equally applicable to Fire Insurance, Burglary Insurance, and all other general (non-life) indemnity contracts. It has been adopted as a general principle of insurance law in India.

Hardy Ivamy — Doctrinal Definition

"Double insurance exists where the same person insures the same interest in the same subject matter against the same risks with two or more different insurers."

I
The Five Essential Elements of Double Insurance
Critical Examination Point

ALL FIVE elements must coincide simultaneously for double insurance to exist. If even one element is absent — the rules of double insurance do not apply. The fifth element (aggregate exceeds value) is what triggers the practical consequences.

#ElementExplanation & Trap
1 Same Assured Policies must be taken by or on behalf of the same person. If A insures his house AND B (the mortgagee) separately insures the same house — different assured persons, different interests — this is NOT double insurance in the strict sense.
2 Same Subject Matter Subject matter insured must be identical. If one policy covers a warehouse and another covers goods inside the warehouse — different subject matters — NO double insurance.
3 Same Risk / Same Peril Both policies must cover the same peril. If one policy covers fire and another covers burglary on the same property — different perils covered — NO double insurance.
4 Same Interest Both policies must cover the same insurable interest. If A (owner) and B (mortgagee) both insure the same house against fire — they have different insurable interests — A has owner's interest, B has mortgagee's interest. NOT double insurance.
5 ★ Aggregate Sum Exceeds Value The combined sums insured under all policies must exceed the actual value or actual loss. This is what triggers the practical consequences of double insurance rules. If all five elements are present but aggregate does NOT exceed actual value — insured can recover fully from one or proportionately from each insurer without the complications of double insurance.
II
Legal Consequences of Double Insurance — Section 34(2), MIA 1963
⚖
Section 34(2) — Complete Framework
Marine Insurance Act, 1963
▾
Sub-clauseRule
§ 34(2)(a) — Order of Claims "The assured, unless the policy otherwise provides, may claim payment from the insurers in such order as he may think fit, provided that he is not entitled to receive any sum in excess of the indemnity allowed by this Act." — Insured chooses which insurer to approach first. Total recovery cannot exceed actual loss.
§ 34(2)(b) — Valued Policy Credit Rule "Where the policy under which the assured claims is a valued policy, the assured must give credit as against the valuation, for any sum received by him under any other policy, without regard to the actual value of the subject-matter insured." — If agreed value = ₹10 lakh and assured collects ₹6 lakh from Insurer A → can claim only ₹4 lakh from Insurer B.
§ 34(2)(c) — Unvalued Policy Credit Rule "Where the policy under which the assured claims is an unvalued policy, he must give credit, as against the full insurable value, for any sum received by him under any other policy." — Credit given against full insurable value.
§ 34(2)(d) ★ — Excess Held in Trust "Where the assured receives any sum in excess of the indemnity allowed by this Act, he is deemed to hold such sum in trust for the insurers, according to their right of contribution among themselves." — The insured holds excess NOT as his own property but as trustee for the insurers. This prevents unjust enrichment with legal precision.

§ 84(2)(f) MIA 1963 — Return of Premium in Double Insurance

"Where the assured has over-insured by double insurance, a proportionate part of the several premiums is returnable: Provided that if the policies are effected at different times, and any earlier policy has at any time borne the entire risk, or if a claim has been paid on the policy in respect of the full sum insured thereby, no premium is returnable in respect of that policy."

So if double insurance detected before any claim — excess premium on the later policy may be refunded proportionately. But once a claim has been paid on a policy for the full sum — no refund is possible on that policy.

III
The Right of Contribution — Section 80, MIA 1963
Section 80(1) — Marine Insurance Act, 1963

"Where the assured is over-insured by double insurance, each insurer is bound, as between himself and the other insurers, to contribute rateably to the loss in proportion to the amount for which he is liable under his contract."

Section 80(2)

"If any insurer pays more than his proportion of the loss, he is entitled to maintain a suit for contribution against the other insurers, and is entitled to the like remedies as a surety who has paid more than his proportion of the debt."

Contribution Formula
Each Insurer's Liability = (That Insurer's Sum Insured ÷ Total Sum Insured Across All Policies) × Total Loss
Illustration 1: Property worth ₹10,00,000 · Policy with Insurer A = ₹6,00,000 · Policy with Insurer B = ₹4,00,000 · Total cover = ₹10,00,000 · Loss = ₹5,00,000
A's share = (6/10) × 5,00,000 = ₹3,00,000 · B's share = (4/10) × 5,00,000 = ₹2,00,000
If insured claims ₹5,00,000 entirely from A → A recovers ₹2,00,000 from B by contribution.

Illustration 2 (M.S. Rama Rao): Smt. Rani's property insured with A for ₹30,000 and B for ₹20,000. Actual value = ₹80,000. Loss = ₹25,000.
A's liability = (30,000/80,000) × 25,000 = ₹9,375 · B's liability = (20,000/80,000) × 25,000 = ₹6,250
Balance ₹9,375 borne by Smt. Rani as her own insurer (under-insured by ₹30,000).

Conditions for the Right of Contribution

  • Both policies must cover the same subject matter.
  • Both policies must cover the same interest of the same assured.
  • Both policies must cover the same peril which caused the loss.
  • Both policies must be legally valid and enforceable at the time of loss. A void or voidable policy cannot contribute.
  • Both policies must be in force at the time of loss.
⚖ North British and Mercantile Insurance Co. v. London, Liverpool and Globe Insurance Co. (1877) 5 Ch D 569 — LEADING AUTHORITY

Two fire insurance companies both insured the same warehouse. Fire occurred. Held: Both companies liable to contribute proportionately. Neither could avoid contribution by arguing the other policy was "primary." Established: Contribution operates on the principle of equality — each insurer bears the loss proportionate to the sum for which it is liable. The pro-rata contribution method is established.

⚖ Albion Fire and Life Insurance Co. v. Mills (1828) — FOUNDATIONAL CASE

One of the foundational English cases establishing that contribution exists as an equitable right between co-insurers. The right exists NOT because of any contractual arrangement between the insurers — but because it would be unconscionable for one insurer to bear more than its fair share.

IV
Life Insurance — The Critical Exception
Most Commonly Examined Distinction — Life Insurance & Double Insurance

Life Insurance is NOT subject to double insurance rules in the same way as general insurance.

Because life insurance is NOT a contract of indemnity (established in Dalby v. India and London Assurance Co. (1854) — House of Lords) — the insured can take multiple life policies and claim the full amount under EACH upon death or maturity. There is NO "over-insurance" concept in life insurance.

Example: If A takes five life policies of ₹10 lakh each — upon his death, his nominees can claim ₹10 lakh from EACH — totaling ₹50 lakh — regardless of any "actual value" of life (which is legally incapable of precise valuation).

"Other Insurance" Clauses in Policies

In practice, insurers protect themselves against consequences of double insurance by including "Other Insurance" clauses (also called Rateable Proportion Clauses) in their policies.

Typical Other Insurance Clause

"If at the time of any loss or damage happening to any property hereby insured, there shall be any other subsisting insurance or insurances covering the same property, the Company shall not be liable to pay or contribute more than its rateable proportion of such loss or damage."

Such clauses are legally valid and enforceable — confirmed in numerous Indian and English cases. They prevent the insured from claiming the full loss from one insurer and force proportionate recovery from each.

Reinsurance — Comprehensive Lecture
§ 2(16B)
Definition — Section 2(16B), Insurance Act, 1938 (inserted by Act 5 of 2015)
Section 2(16B) — Insurance Act, 1938 — Statutory Definition (MEMORIZE VERBATIM)

"Re-insurance means the insurance of part of one insurer's risk by another insurer who accepts the risk for a mutually acceptable premium."

Section 11(1) — Marine Insurance Act, 1963 — Insurable Interest of Cedant

"The insurer under a contract of marine insurance has an insurable interest in his risk, and may re-insure in respect of it."

Section 11(2) — Marine Insurance Act, 1963 — CRITICAL PROVISION

"Unless the policy otherwise provides, the original assured has no right or interest in respect of such reinsurance."

Section 11(2) — What It Means

The original insured has absolutely NO rights against the reinsurer. The relationship is exclusively between the ceding insurer and the reinsurer. If the reinsurer becomes insolvent — the original insured's claims against the cedant are completely unaffected. The cedant must still pay the original insured from its own resources.

The Conceptual Logic of Reinsurance — Why Does an Insurer Need Insurance?

Consider: An insurer issues a policy covering a large oil refinery for ₹500 crore. If the refinery burns down, the insurer must pay ₹500 crore. No single insurance company may have the financial capacity to absorb such a catastrophic loss.

Solution: The insurer passes part of the risk to a reinsurer — say, retains ₹50 crore and passes ₹450 crore to reinsurers.

What Reinsurance AchievesExplanation
Financial Solvency ProtectionIndividual insurers protect themselves against catastrophic losses that could bankrupt them.
Risk DistributionLarge risks are distributed across the global insurance and reinsurance market.
Capacity CreationInsurers can accept larger risks than their capital alone would permit.
Price StabilityAchieves stability in the insurance market — catastrophic losses do not destabilize insurers.

Parties to Reinsurance

PartyRole
Original InsuredPerson who takes the original insurance policy. Has NO rights against the reinsurer (§ 11(2) MIA 1963).
Cedant / Ceding CompanyThe original insurer who accepts the risk from the original insured and then passes part of it to the reinsurer. Also called "direct insurer."
ReinsurerThe insurer who accepts the transferred risk from the cedant in exchange for reinsurance premium.
RetrocessionaireA reinsurer who further reinsures its own accepted risk with yet another reinsurer. This creates a global chain of risk distribution.
§§ 101A–101C
Reinsurance in Indian Statutory Law — Part IVA, Insurance Act, 1938
The Dedicated Legislative Framework for Reinsurance in India

Part IVA of the Insurance Act, 1938 — Sections 101A, 101B, 101C — inserted by the Insurance Act Amendment of 1961 — is the dedicated legislative framework for reinsurance in India. These sections carry significant examination weight.

§
Section 101A — Re-insurance with Indian Re-insurers
The Mandatory Cession Provision
▾
Section 101A(1)

"Every insurer shall re-insure with Indian re-insurers such percentage of the sum assured on each policy as may be specified by the Authority with the previous approval of the Central Government under sub-section (2)."

What this means: Every insurer operating in India is obligatorily required to reinsure a portion of its risk with Indian reinsurers. This is MANDATORY — not optional. The purpose is to retain reinsurance premiums within India rather than allowing them to flow out to foreign reinsurers.

Sub-sectionContent
§ 101A(2)(i)IRDAI may specify the percentage of sum assured to be reinsured. Different percentages may be specified for different classes of insurance. Proviso: Maximum cap = 30% of the sum assured on any policy. This cap prevents excessive mandatory reinsurance that would hamper the original insurer's capacity.
§ 101A(2)(ii)IRDAI may specify the proportions in which the mandatory percentage shall be allocated among Indian re-insurers.
§ 101A(3)Special provision for fire insurance: Insurer may reinsure with Indian reinsurers such amount out of the first surplus in respect of that business — provided aggregate reinsurance premiums in any year are not less than the specified percentage of premium income.
§ 101A(4)Notification under sub-section (2) may also specify terms and conditions of reinsurance business — binding on both Indian reinsurers and other insurers.
§ 101A(5)No notification under sub-section (2) shall be issued except after consultation with the Advisory Committee constituted under Section 101B.
§ 101A(6)Every notification shall be laid before each House of Parliament — parliamentary oversight mechanism.
§ 101A(7)Clarificatory: Nothing in sub-section (1) prevents an insurer from additionally reinsuring the entire sum assured or any portion thereof with any Indian reinsurer. Mandatory reinsurance sets a floor — voluntary additional reinsurance is always permitted.
§ 101A(8)(i)"Policy" = policy issued or renewed on or after 1st April 1961, in respect of general insurance business transacted in India. Does NOT include a reinsurance policy.
§ 101A(8)(ii)"Indian Re-insurer" = an Indian insurance company granted a certificate of registration under sub-section (2A) of Section 3 by the Authority to carry on EXCLUSIVELY the reinsurance business in India. [Substituted by Insurance (Amendment) Act, 2002, s.15 — w.e.f. 23-9-2002.]

§
Section 101C — Examination of Reinsurance Treaties
IRDAI Regulatory Oversight
▾

The Authority may at any time:

  • Call upon an insurer to submit for examination at its principal place of business in India — all reinsurance treaties and other reinsurance contracts entered into by the insurer.
  • Examine any officer of the insurer on oath in relation to any such document.
  • By notice in writing, require any insurer to supply copies of any such documents, certified by a principal officer.

Purpose: Regulatory oversight — IRDAI ensures that reinsurance arrangements are commercially sound, not designed to siphon premiums abroad in violation of Indian law, and are not prejudicial to policyholders' interests.

Connection to Section 34F of Insurance Act

Section 34F gives the Authority power to issue directions regarding reinsurance treaties if it finds the terms or conditions are NOT favorable to the insurer or detrimental to public interest. The Authority can direct modifications or even non-renewal of such treaties.

Nature of Reinsurance Contract
CharacteristicContent
1. Insurance of InsuranceLiterally the "insurance of insurance" — governed by the same fundamental principles as any insurance contract (utmost good faith, insurable interest, indemnity).
2. Insurable Interest (§ 11(1))The cedant (original insurer) has an insurable interest in the risk it has underwritten. The financial loss the cedant would suffer if the original claim materializes = its insurable interest.
3. Uberrima FidesReinsurance contracts are governed by utmost good faith — arguably even more stringently than direct insurance, because: (a) reinsurer has no direct relationship with original insured; (b) reinsurer cannot independently verify the risk; (c) reinsurer relies entirely on the cedant's information. Cedant must disclose ALL material facts about the original risk.
4. Indemnity PrincipleReinsurance is a contract of indemnity — the reinsurer indemnifies the cedant against the loss the cedant suffers when it pays the original insured's claim. Reinsurer's liability triggered only when cedant actually pays or becomes legally obligated to pay.
5. "Follow the Fortunes" DoctrineThe reinsurer generally agrees to "follow the fortunes" of the cedant — if the cedant settles a claim in good faith, the reinsurer is bound to follow that settlement even if it might have contested the claim differently. Reflects the interdependence of cedant and reinsurer.
6. Separate and Independent ContractReinsurance contract entirely separate from the original insurance policy. Original insured has NO privity with reinsurer. Confirmed by § 11(2) MIA 1963. If reinsurer becomes insolvent — original insured's claims against cedant are unaffected.

Types of Reinsurance — Complete Classification

Classification A — Based on Method of Arrangement

Arrangement-Based · Automatic
1. Treaty Reinsurance
Definition: Standing arrangement — reinsurer agrees in advance to accept a specified class or category of risks automatically, without case-by-case negotiation.

Nature: Long-term, automatic, obligatory.

Types:
• Obligatory Treaty: Both parties bound — cedant must cede, reinsurer must accept.
• Facultative-Obligatory (Fac-Ob): Cedant may choose to cede or not, but if it cedes, reinsurer must accept.

Advantages: Automatic cover without delay · Economical (saves admin costs) · Provides continuous, predictable protection.

Disadvantages: Reinsurer exposed to adverse selection (bears whatever cedant underwrites) · Less flexibility in pricing individual risks.
Arrangement-Based · Individual
2. Facultative Reinsurance
Definition: Case-by-case arrangement — cedant offers specific risks; reinsurer has option to accept or decline each individual risk on its own merits.

Nature: Individual, discretionary, negotiated.

When used: Exceptional, large, or unusual risks outside treaty coverage. Each risk separately underwritten and priced.

Example: Policy for a nuclear power plant worth ₹1,000 crore — no treaty covers this automatically. Insurer must approach reinsurers facultatively, one by one, to build up required capacity.

Advantages: Reinsurer can select good risks · Flexibility in pricing · Suitable for unique, high-value risks.

Disadvantages: Time-consuming — delay in confirmation of cover · Expensive — high admin cost per transaction · No guarantee of capacity availability.

Classification B — Based on Structure of Risk Sharing

📊
1. Proportional Reinsurance
Premium AND losses shared in same agreed proportion
▾

Cardinal Principle: Whatever ratio is agreed for sharing premium — the SAME ratio applies to sharing losses.

Proportional Type 1
(a) Quota Share Treaty
Cedant and reinsurer agree to share ALL risks in a fixed, pre-determined percentage — regardless of the size of the risk.

Illustration: Quota Share: 60% cedant, 40% reinsurer. Policy premium = ₹1,00,000 · Loss = ₹80,000 → Cedant retains ₹60,000 premium and pays ₹48,000 of loss. Reinsurer receives ₹40,000 premium and pays ₹32,000 of loss.

Advantage: Simple, automatic, immediate protection.
Disadvantage: Cedant gives away share of profit on small, safe risks unnecessarily.
Proportional Type 2
(b) Surplus Treaty ("Excess of Line")
Cedant first retains a defined maximum amount (called the "line" or "retention") on each risk. Only the surplus over the retention is ceded — in proportion to the total sum insured.

M.S. Rama Rao Illustration: Oriental company issues policy for ₹30 lakhs. Retains ₹20 lakhs, transfers risk for ₹10 lakhs to New India Assurance. Premium proportionately shared. Insured is NOT a party to this arrangement.

Advantage: Cedant retains profitable smaller risks fully. More rational distribution. Cedant exercises underwriting judgment.
📈
2. Non-Proportional Reinsurance
Premium and losses NOT shared in same ratio — reinsurer pays only excess above retention
▾

Principle: The cedant bears all losses up to its retention (priority). The reinsurer kicks in ONLY above that level up to an agreed limit.

Non-Proportional Type 1 · Most Common
(a) Excess of Loss (XL) Reinsurance
Reinsurer pays losses that exceed the cedant's retention up to an agreed limit — per individual risk or occurrence.

Illustration: Cedant's retention = ₹50 lakh. Reinsurer's limit = ₹2 crore xs ₹50 lakh.
Loss of ₹1.5 crore → Cedant pays ₹50 lakh · Reinsurer pays ₹1 crore.
Loss of ₹3 crore → Cedant pays ₹50 lakh · Reinsurer pays ₹2 crore (max) · Balance ₹50 lakh = uninsured.

Types: Per Risk XL (each individual loss) · Per Occurrence XL (cumulative losses from single event — earthquake, flood).
Advantage: Most efficient protection against large individual losses.
Non-Proportional Type 2
(b) Stop Loss (Aggregate Excess of Loss)
Reinsurer pays when the AGGREGATE losses for the entire year exceed a specified percentage of the cedant's premium income.

Illustration: Stop Loss trigger = 80% of annual premium income. Annual premium = ₹100 crore. Trigger point = ₹80 crore in losses. If total losses = ₹95 crore → Cedant bears ₹80 crore · Reinsurer pays ₹15 crore.

Use: Protects against catastrophic years — widespread floods, pandemics, etc. Portfolio-wide protection.

Additional Key Concepts

ConceptExplanation
RetrocessionA reinsurer itself reinsures part of the risk it accepted from the cedant — with yet another reinsurer called the Retrocessionaire. Chain: Original Insured → Insurer (Cedant) → Reinsurer → Retrocessionaire. Allows global distribution of catastrophic risk. § 101A prevents all risk from being transferred outside India through retrocession chains.
Ceding CommissionWhen insurer cedes risk to reinsurer under proportional treaties, the reinsurer pays back a Ceding Commission to the cedant — a percentage of the reinsurance premium — to cover cedant's acquisition costs and admin expenses. Flows FROM reinsurer TO cedant (distinct from insurance agent commission which flows from insurer to agent).
GIC ReGeneral Insurance Corporation of India — India's national reinsurer. Established under GIBNA 1972. Converted from holding company of four public sector insurers into dedicated reinsurer in 2000. First recipient of mandatory cessions from Indian insurers.

The Nine Pillars — Connecting Everything

1
Uberrima Fides
Universal. Applies to ALL insurance. Carter v. Boehm (1766) — Lord Mansfield: "The insurer knows nothing; the assured knows everything." § 19 MIA codifies it. § 45 Insurance Act limits challenges after 3 years for life insurance.
2
Insurable Interest
Universal — timing varies. Fire = at policy AND loss. Marine = at loss (§ 8(1)). Life = at commencement only (Dalby). Macaura v. Northern Assurance — sole shareholder has no interest in company assets.
3
Indemnity
Fire, Marine, Misc = YES. Life = NO. Restore insured to pre-loss position. Castellain v. Preston (1883). Exceptions: valued policies in marine; life insurance; personal accident.
4
Proximate Cause
Universal. Causa proxima non remota spectatur. Dominant, direct, operative cause. Symington (preventive damage covered) · Marsden (mob = independent cause) · Tootal (earthquake = proximate, not fire).
5
Subrogation
Fire, Marine, Misc = YES. Life = NO. Insurer steps into insured's shoes after paying. § 79 MIA. Yorkshire v. N.S. Co. — limited to amount paid. "The assured cannot take from both hands."
6
Contribution
General insurance = YES. Life = NO. Multiple policies on same risk — each contributes pro-rata. § 80 MIA. Formula: (Insurer's sum ÷ total sums) × loss. North British v. Liverpool & Globe.
7
Premium
Universal. Consideration for risk. § 64VB — no risk without advance premium. Days of grace (30 days typically). § 113 — policy does not lapse once surrender value acquired (non-linked). Return on fraud = NO; innocent misrepresentation = YES.
8
Warranty
Universal — strictest in Marine. § 35(3) MIA: "must be exactly complied with — whether material to risk or not." Breach = automatic discharge from date of breach. Irremedial (§ 36(2)). Waiver possible (§ 36(3)).
9
Assignment
Life: § 38 (Insurance Act). Marine: § 52 MIA — assignable before OR after loss. Fire: NOT automatically transferred with property. Rule in Dearle v. Hall — priority determined by date of notice to insurer.

The Beautiful Logical Thread — Seeing Everything as One System

Insurance Law is not a collection of disconnected topics — it is one coherent, logical system. Here is the thread that runs through everything:

  • →Start with an ordinary contract → add utmost good faith (because one party knows more than the other) → add insurable interest (to prevent wagering) → the result is a contract of insurance.
  • →If the insurance is for property → add indemnity (you can't profit from loss) → add subrogation (insurer steps into your shoes after paying) → add contribution (multiple policies share loss proportionately).
  • →If multiple policies exist for same property → you have double insurance.
  • →If the risk is too large for one insurer → they share it with another insurer → you have reinsurance.
  • →And governing all of this — watching over the entire ecosystem → IRDAI (established under IRDA Act 1999 · Section 14 functions · Registration · Policyholder protection · Investment regulation · Social sector obligations).
"Every principle, every section number, every case judgment in Insurance Law ultimately serves one master purpose: To create a mechanism where the financial burden of individual misfortune is distributed across many shoulders, while preventing that mechanism from being abused for profit or gaming." — The Unifying Philosophy of Insurance Law

All Leading Cases — Complete Master Reference

Carter v. Boehm (1766)
Foundation of uberrima fides — "insurer knows nothing; assured knows everything" — Lord Mansfield
Dalby v. India & London (1854)
Life insurance is NOT a contract of indemnity — House of Lords — insurable interest at commencement only
Macaura v. Northern Assurance (1925)
Sole shareholder has NO insurable interest in company's property
Castellain v. Preston (1883)
Indemnity principle; leading case on subrogation — insured cannot take from both hands
LIC v. Shakuntala Devi
Trivial ailments (indigestion) not material facts — LIC held liable
Godfrey v. Brittanic Assurance
Serious health conditions (lung infection, kidney, pharyngitis) are material — company not liable
Griffith v. Fleming
Insurable interest between spouses is presumed by law
Cleaver v. Mutual Reserve
Beneficiary who caused insured's death cannot recover; executors can
Sarbati Devi v. Usha Devi (SC)
Nominee = "hand to receive" only — modified by § 39(7)&(8) for close family
Beresford v. Royal Insurance (1938)
English law on suicide — felo de se; Lord Atkin: claim rejected. Indian position differs.
Kankayalal's Case
Suicide after 1-year policy exclusion period — claim upheld. Indian law.
Harris v. Poland
Friendly fire causing accidental loss to unintended items = COVERED
Thompson's Case
Exclusion clauses construed strictly (contra proferentem) against insurer
Tootal v. London Fire Ins.
Earthquake = proximate cause of fire; fire insurer NOT liable
Marsden v. City Insurance
Mob lawlessness = independent intervening cause; fire insurer not liable for glass
Symington v. Union Insurance
Acts to prevent fire spreading — covered as proximate fire loss
Yorkshire Ins. v. Nisbet Shipping
Subrogation limited to amount paid by insurer — not entitled to excess
North British v. Liverpool & Globe (1877)
Contribution — pro-rata method — each insurer bears proportionate share
Dearle v. Hall
Priority of assignees determined by date of notice to insurer — not date of assignment
Anderson v. Morice
Marine insurable interest at time of loss — loading not complete = no interest
Asfar v. Blundell
Subject matter ceasing to be "thing of kind insured" = actual total loss
Vacuum Oil v. Union Insurance
Constructive total loss — prohibitive cost of forwarding damaged cargo
Rickards v. Forestal
Government-ordered scuttling = constructive total loss — insurer liable
Hall v. Hayman
Wreck value NOT taken into account in CTL calculation
Samuel v. Dumas
Scuttling with owner's connivance — not covered; dominant cause test
Thomas v. Hamilton (Inchmaree)
Valve failure due to negligence — not covered by standard perils; led to Inchmaree Clause
Baxendale v. Fane
Negligent docking = covered under Inchmaree clause
Gaunt's Case
All risks — insured proves fortuitous loss; need not identify specific cause
Elliot v. Wilson
Deviation at unauthorized port = insurer discharged
National Insurance v. J. Nadar
Jettison to save human life = excused deviation — insurer liable
Swaran Singh (2004) 3 SCC 297
Fundamental vs. technical breach — motor insurance; structured framework for § 149
MC Mehta v. Union of India (1987)
Absolute liability for hazardous enterprises — beyond Rylands v. Fletcher
Rikhi Ram v. Sukhrania (2003)
Motor policy automatically transferred to vehicle transferee — § 157 MV Act
Forsikringsaktieselskapet Vesta v. Butcher (1989)
Reinsurance back-to-back with direct insurance — reinsurer's liability mirrors cedant's
