Insurance — Definition, Essentials, Nature & Kinds
The intellectual heart of Insurance Law

1.1 Definitions of Insurance

There is no single omnibus statutory definition of "insurance" in the Insurance Act, 1938. The Act defines specific types — life (§ 2(11)), fire (§ 2(6A)), marine (§ 2(13A)), general (§ 2(6B)) — but not "insurance" itself. We must rely on judicial and scholarly definitions.

Prudential Insurance Company v. Inland Revenue Commissioner (1904) — Channel J.

"There must be either some uncertainty whether the event will ever happen or not, or if the event is one which must happen at some time or another, there must be uncertainty as to the time at which it will happen."

Cotton L.J. in Castellain v. Preston (1883)

"A contract whereby one person undertakes in return for agreed consideration to indemnify another person against loss or damage occasioned by fire up to the agreed amount." [Speaking on fire insurance; also the leading case on subrogation.]

Hardy Ivamy — General Principles of Insurance Law

"A contract of insurance is a contract whereby one person called the insurer, undertakes in return for the agreed consideration called the premium, to pay to another person called the insured, a sum of money or its equivalent on the happening of a specified event."

Lawrence J. in Lucena v. Craufurd (1806) — landmark case on insurable interest

"A man is interested in a thing to whom advantage may arise or prejudice happen from the circumstances which may attend it."

Synthesized Definition for Examination

Insurance is a contract of utmost good faith between the insurer and the insured, in which the insured, having an insurable interest in the subject matter, pays a premium as consideration, and the insurer undertakes to indemnify the insured (or pay a stipulated sum) against loss arising from an uncertain event (or certain event of uncertain time) covered by the policy, thereby distributing the risk of financial loss over a large number of similarly situated individuals.

1.2 Essentials of a Valid Contract of Insurance

A. General Essentials (Indian Contract Act, 1872)

  • Offer & Acceptance — Proposal form = offer; insurer's acceptance (after medical report, premium, agent's report) = acceptance. Policy document is evidence.
  • Free Consent — Free from coercion (§ 15), undue influence (§ 16), fraud (§ 17), misrepresentation (§ 18), mistake (§ 20–22). In insurance, uberrima fides raises the standard far beyond ordinary contract law.
  • Competency of Parties — Both must satisfy § 11 Contract Act: majority (18 yrs), sound mind, not disqualified by law.
  • Lawful Consideration — Premium = insured's consideration. Promise to indemnify/pay = insurer's consideration. Both must be lawful.
  • Lawful Object — Insurance on illegal adventures is void. § 43 MIA 1963 — implied warranty of legality.
  • Not Expressly Void — Wagering insurance (without insurable interest) = void under § 30 Contract Act.

B. Special Essentials of Insurance Contracts

Causa ProximaLoss must be proximately caused by a covered peril. Remote causes don't give rise to liability.

02
Doctrine of Utmost Good Faith — Uberrima Fides
§§ 19–22 Marine Insurance Act, 1963 · § 45 Insurance Act, 1938

2.1 Introduction & Rationale

In ordinary contracts, the governing maxim is caveat emptor (let the buyer beware) — parties deal at arm's length and need not volunteer information. In insurance, this principle is completely reversed. The insurer knows nothing about the specific risk; the insured knows everything. The entire actuarial foundation of insurance would collapse if insurers could not rely on information from proposers.

Core Principle

The duty of utmost good faith is mutual — it binds both the insured AND the insurer. Breach entitles the innocent party to avoid the contract (treat it as never made).

2.2 The Foundational Case — Carter v. Boehm (1766)

⚖ Carter v. Boehm (1766) — Lord Mansfield — LEADING CASE

Facts: Carter, Governor of Fort Marlborough (Sumatra), insured the fort against capture by foreign enemy. French invaded and captured it. Insurer (Boehm) refused — arguing Carter should have disclosed (i) weakness of the fort and (ii) likelihood of French attack.

Held: Carter succeeded. These facts were within the knowledge of a prudent insurer — matters of common geopolitical knowledge. Lord Mansfield: "Insurance is a contract upon speculation. The special facts... lie most commonly in the knowledge of the insured only: the underwriter trusts to his representation, and proceeds upon confidence that he does not keep back any circumstance in his knowledge."

Rule: The standard of materiality is what a prudent insurer would consider material — objective, not what the insured personally believes.

2.3 Legal Basis of the Duty

The duty does not arise from an implied term, tort, or fiduciary relationship. It arises from the sui generis (unique) nature of insurance contracts. In India, the duty is embedded in:

Marine Insurance
§§ 19, 20, 21 — Marine Insurance Act, 1963
Life Insurance
§ 45 — Insurance Act, 1938
General Contract Law
§ 17 (fraud) & § 18 (misrepresentation) — Indian Contract Act, 1872

2.4–2.6 The Marine Insurance Act Sections on Disclosure

§
§ 19 — Insurance is Uberrimae Fidei (Marine Insurance Act, 1963)
The Statutory Foundation
▾

"A contract of marine insurance is a contract based upon the utmost good faith, and if the utmost good faith be not observed by either party, the contract may be avoided by the other party."

This is the statutory embodiment of Carter v. Boehm. Note: duty is mutual; remedy is avoidance.

§
§ 20 — Disclosure by the Assured
The Prudent Insurer Test
▾

§ 20(1): The assured must disclose to the insurer, before the contract is concluded, every material circumstance known to the assured. The assured is deemed to know every circumstance which, in the ordinary course of business, ought to be known to him.

§ 20(2) — Test of Materiality: "Every circumstance is material which would influence the judgment of a prudent insurer in fixing the premium, or determining whether he will take the risk." — This is an objective test, not the insured's personal view.

§ 20(3) — What Need NOT be Disclosed

  • Any circumstance which diminishes the risk — a fact reducing risk need not be disclosed.
  • Any circumstance known or presumed to be known to the insurer — insurer presumed to know matters of common notoriety and ordinary business knowledge.
  • Any circumstance as to which information is waived by the insurer.
  • Any circumstance that is superfluous to disclose by reason of any express or implied warranty.
§
§ 21 — Disclosure by Agent
Knowledge of Agent = Knowledge of Principal
▾

Where insurance is effected through an agent, the agent must disclose: (a) every material circumstance known to himself; (b) every material circumstance the assured is bound to disclose, unless it comes to the agent's knowledge too late.

⚖ Bawden v. London, Edinburgh & Glasgow Assurance Co. (1892)

Agent had knowledge that the assured had only one eye (material for accident insurance). Proposal form did not record this. Held: agent's knowledge imputed to the insurer — insurer liable.

⚖ Wing v. Harvey

Agent knowingly accepted premium with knowledge of untrue statement in proposal. Held: insurer estopped from denying liability.

2.8 Indian Cases on Utmost Good Faith

⚖ LIC v. Shakuntala Devi — TRIVIAL AILMENTS NEED NOT BE DISCLOSED

LIC contended insured had not disclosed indigestion for a few days before policy. Insured died within a year. Held: Indigestion, headache, etc. are casual disturbances of health — not material facts. LIC cannot take advantage. LIC held liable. Principle: Trivial/temporary ailments of no actuarial significance need not be disclosed.

⚖ Godfrey v. Brittanic Assurance Co. — SERIOUS CONDITIONS ARE MATERIAL

Assured did not disclose lung infection, kidney problem, and pharyngitis while signing proposal. Held: These were material facts — company not liable.

⚖ Lambert's Case — CRIMINAL RECORD IS MATERIAL

W took policy on jewellery. Husband H had been convicted twice for dishonesty — not disclosed. Held: Material fact — moral hazard arising from H's criminal record highly material. Company not liable.

⚖ Ratanlal v. M.I. Co. — MOMENTARY EXHAUSTION NOT MATERIAL

Insured had momentary exhaustion on the same day policy was issued. Doctor examined but gave no medicine. Insured died April 19. Held: Not a material fact. Company held liable.

2.9 The Duty is Mutual

⚖ Banque Financiere de la Cite SA v. Westgate Insurance Co. Ltd.

Confirmed mutual nature of the duty. "The remedy to the insurer for the insured's breach is to avoid the contract; the remedy to the insured for the insurer's breach is also to avoid the contract."

  • Life policy cannot be questioned on any ground after 3 years from date of policy / commencement of risk / revival / rider (whichever is later).

Doctrine of Indemnity — Subrogation, Contribution & Reinstatement
§§ 67–82 MIA 1963 · Average Clause · Standard Fire Policy

3.1 The Principle & Its Rationale

"The insured shall be placed in the same financial position after a loss as they occupied immediately before the loss — no better, no worse." — Principle of Indemnity
⚖ Castellain v. Preston (1883) — Cotton L.J. — FOUNDATIONAL CASE

"The fundamental principle of insurance law is that the contract of insurance contained in a marine or fire policy is a contract of indemnity, and of indemnity only." The court also established subrogation — the insured must account to the insurer for sums received from third parties that reduce their actual loss.

Rationale — Two Pillars: (1) Prevention of unjust enrichment — if insured could profit from loss, people would be incentivized to cause the very losses they are insured against. (2) Preservation of the actuarial basis — premiums are calculated on compensation of actual losses, not profit opportunities.

3.2 Measure of Indemnity

Insurance TypeMeasure of Indemnity
Fire InsuranceMarket value of damaged property at time and place of loss, subject to sum insured. Market value = cost of construction/replacement less depreciation for age, usage, wear and tear. Cannot recover anticipated profits, goodwill, or sentimental value.
Marine Insurance§ 17 MIA 1963 defines insurable value precisely for ships, freight, goods. For total loss under valued policy — agreed value conclusive (§ 29(3)). Measure of indemnity detailed in §§ 67–78.
Life InsuranceNo fixed measure — agreed sum assured is paid without reference to actual financial loss. Hence not a contract of indemnity. Dalby v. India & London Assurance Co. (1854).

3.3 Exceptions to the Principle of Indemnity

  • Life Insurance — Not a contract of indemnity. Dalby v. India and London Assurance Co. (1854): House of Lords held payment is made on death or maturity without proof of loss.
  • Personal Accident & Sickness Insurance — Fixed sum paid on happening of specified event; not proportional to financial loss.
  • Valued Policies (Marine Insurance) — § 28 & § 29(3) MIA 1963: agreed value at outset is conclusive. If full agreed value recovered even though market value lower at time of loss — technically exceeds indemnity.

3.4 Doctrines Flowing from Indemnity

→
(i) Subrogation
§ 79 Marine Insurance Act, 1963
▾

Definition: Subrogation is the right of the insurer, having indemnified the insured for a loss, to step into the shoes of the insured and exercise whatever rights the insured has against third parties responsible for the loss.

Basis: If insurer pays AND insured also recovers from the responsible third party, the insured makes a profit — violating indemnity. Subrogation prevents this.

Justice Mansfield: "Every day the insurer is put in the place of the insured." The courts: "The assured cannot take from both hands."

⚖ Castellain v. Preston (1883) — THE LEADING SUBROGATION CASE

A agreed to sell house to B (B to pay unconditionally). A's house (insured) was destroyed by fire. Insurance company paid total loss. B also paid A under the sale contract. Insurance company claimed the purchase money paid by B. Held: Insurance company entitled — insured cannot keep both insurance money and sale proceeds for the same loss.

⚖ Yorkshire v. N.S. Co. — LIMIT OF SUBROGATION

Canadian Government vessel caused total loss to Vessel A. Insurer paid ₹72,000. Owner later sued Canadian Government and got ₹1,27,000. Insurer sued to recover. Held: Insurer entitled to recover only ₹72,000 — the amount it had paid — not more.

⚖ Glen Line Case — INSURER GETS RECOVERED WRECK

Ship A insured for £10,000, lost in maritime peril, insurer paid total loss. Ship later recovered from seabed and sold. Held: Insurer entitled to the sale value — subrogation entitles insurer to whatever remains of the subject matter.

Key Principles of Subrogation

  • Right arises only upon actual payment of the total loss to the insured.
  • Insurer gets the rights of the assured when the latter has a right of action against third parties.
  • Insurer's claim is limited to what he has paid — not more.
  • NOT applicable to life insurance, personal accident insurance, or any non-indemnity insurance.
  • Insured must not compromise or settle rights against third parties in a manner that defeats the insurer's subrogation rights.
⚖
(ii) Contribution
§ 80 Marine Insurance Act, 1963 · Average Clause
▾

Definition: Where the insured has taken out two or more insurance policies covering the same risk and interest, and a loss occurs, each insurer contributes rateably to the loss. No insurer pays more than their proportionate share; insured cannot recover more than the actual loss in total.

Mathematical Formula

Contribution Formula
Each Insurer's Share = (That Insurer's Sum Insured ÷ Total Insured Amount) × Amount of Loss
Example: Property value ₹80,000. Insured with A for ₹30,000, with B for ₹20,000. Loss: ₹25,000.
A's share = (30,000 ÷ 80,000) × 25,000 = ₹9,375
B's share = (20,000 ÷ 80,000) × 25,000 = ₹6,250
Balance ₹9,375 borne by insured (under-insurer by ₹30,000 of actual value).

Average Clause (Condition 10, Standard Fire Policy)

Penalizes under-insurance. If the insured has insured for less than the property's value, they bear a proportionate share as their own insurer.

Average Clause Formula
Amount Payable = (Sum Insured ÷ Actual Value of Property) × Amount of Loss
Example: Building insured for ₹1,50,000; actual value ₹2,50,000; fire loss ₹90,000.
Amount payable = (1,50,000 ÷ 2,50,000) × 90,000 = ₹54,000
Balance ₹36,000 borne by insured. Exception: If sum insured ≥ 85% of actual value, clause does not apply.
Double Insurance — Section 34 MIA, 1963

§ 34(1): Where two or more policies are effected on the same adventure and interest, and sums insured exceed the indemnity allowed — the assured is over-insured by double insurance. The insured may claim from insurers in such order as he thinks fit — but cannot receive more than the indemnity allowed. Excess received is held in trust for the insurers according to their right of contribution.

🏗
(iii) Reinstatement
Clause 7 — Standard Fire Policy
▾

Definition: Instead of paying the loss in cash, the insurer has the option to reinstate or replace the property damaged or destroyed, effectively restoring it to its pre-loss condition.

Statutory Basis: Clause 7 of the Standard Fire Policy (as approved by the Tariff Advisory Committee) gives the insurer this option.

If the insurer elects to reinstate, they must put the premises substantially as they were before the fire. Failure to properly reinstate = liability for damages for the failure. Maximum liability under reinstatement does not exceed the total sum assured.

Why important: Paying the depreciated market value may leave the insured unable to reconstruct. Reinstatement bridges this gap by rebuilding to original condition without deduction for depreciation. Reinstatement Value Policies are designed specifically for this purpose.

Insurable Interest — The Legal Pillar of Insurance
§ 7 Marine Insurance Act, 1963 · Dalby · Macaura · Griffith

4.1 Introduction

Insurable interest is what transforms an insurance contract from a wager into a legitimate commercial transaction. Without it, insurance is mere gambling. No comprehensive statutory definition in the Insurance Act, 1938 — must rely on § 7 MIA 1963 and case law.

§ 7(2) Marine Insurance Act, 1963 — Statutory Definition

"A person is interested in a marine adventure where he stands in any legal or equitable relation to the adventure or to any insurable property at risk therein, in consequence of which he may benefit by the safety or due arrival of insurable property, or may be prejudiced by its loss, or by damage thereto, or by the detention thereof, or may incur liability in respect thereof."

Lawrence J. in Lucena v. Craufurd (1806) — Classic Definition

"A man is interested in a thing to whom advantage may arise or prejudice happen from the circumstances which may attend it... and whom it importeth, that its condition, as to safety or other quality, should continue."

4.2 Essentials of Insurable Interest

  • There must be a subject matter of insurance — property, life, liability, or any other subject.
  • The insured must stand in a defined legal or equitable relationship to the subject matter (ownership, contractual right, statutory right, family relationship, potential liability to third party).
  • The relationship must be such that the insured benefits from safety/survival or suffers loss from damage/destruction.
  • The interest must be legally recognized — enforceable in courts. Purely sentimental interest is insufficient.
  • The interest must be pecuniary (financial) in nature — measurable in money.
  • The interest must not be void on grounds of public policy — criminal interests, interests against national law, interests of enemy alien during war.

4.3 When Must Insurable Interest Exist?

Exception: "lost or not lost" clause — may recover even if interest acquired after loss (unless aware of loss when insuring).Anderson v. M insured his life in wife's favour. Wife poisoned M. W convicted. Held: W was not entitled to recover — "no person shall be allowed to take advantage of their own wrong." However, executors of M's estate could recover and hold it for M's creditors or heirs.

B. In Fire Insurance

⚖ Macaura v. Northern Assurance Co. (1925) — CRITICAL CASE

Facts: Macaura (M) owned timber. Sold it to a company where he was the sole shareholder. Timber insured in M's own name against fire. Timber destroyed by fire. M claimed.
Held: M had NO insurable interest at the time of loss. The timber belonged to the company — not M. As a mere shareholder, M had no legal or equitable interest in company's assets (only in his shares). Claim rejected.
Significance: A shareholder — even a sole shareholder — does NOT have insurable interest in the company's assets. Doctrine of separate corporate personality (Salomon v. Salomon) applies fully in insurance law.

Premium — Definition, Modes, Days of Grace, Forfeiture & Return
§ 64VB Insurance Act, 1938 · § 84 Marine Insurance Act, 1963

5.1 Definition of Premium

Premium is the price or consideration paid by the insured to the insurer for undertaking the risk covered by the insurance policy. It is the insured's side of the contractual bargain.

Section 64VB — No Risk Without Advance Premium

§ 64VB(1): No insurer shall assume any risk in India unless the premium payable is received in advance (or guaranteed to be paid in prescribed manner).
§ 64VB(2) Explanation: Where premium is tendered by postal money order or cheque sent by post, the risk may be assumed on the date on which the money order is booked or the cheque is posted.

5.2 Modes / Methods of Payment of Premium

ModeDescription
Single PremiumEntire premium for full policy term paid in one lump sum at inception. Common in certain endowments, annuities, short-term policies.
Level PremiumRegular periodic installments — annually, semi-annually, quarterly, or monthly — throughout policy term. Amount constant. Most common method.
Limited PremiumPremiums paid for a shorter period than the policy term (e.g., 20-year policy, premiums paid only 10 years).
Natural PremiumTheoretically increases each year as risk increases with age. Used in certain term insurance structures.

§ 64VB(3): Premium refunds shall be paid directly to the insured by crossed cheque or postal money order — NOT credited to agent's account.
§ 64VB(4): Agent who collects premium must deposit/dispatch to insurer in full within 24 hours (excluding bank and postal holidays) — cannot deduct commission first.

5.3 Days of Grace

Days of grace are a concession allowing a specified period beyond the due date during which the policy remains in force and the premium may be paid without affecting validity.

Annual / Quarterly / Semi-Annual
Typically 30 days
Monthly premium policies
Typically 15 days
Death during days of grace?
Insurer IS liable — but unpaid premium deducted from claim

5.4 Forfeiture of Premium

Premium once paid is non-refundable in certain circumstances:

  • Fraud by the insured — if contract avoided due to insured's fraud, premium is forfeited. The fraudster cannot benefit.
  • Where risk has attached — if risk has once attached and policy then avoided for other reason, premium generally not returnable.
  • Wagering contracts — premiums paid under void wagering contracts are not recoverable.

The Risk & Doctrine of Causa Proxima
"Causa proxima non remota spectatur" · § 55 MIA 1963

6.1 Meaning of Risk

Risk in insurance law is a "measurable uncertainty" — a combination of uncertainty (not knowing if/when the harmful event will occur) and potential financial loss (if it does occur). Importantly:

Peril
Source of loss (fire, flood, death) — the event insured against
Hazard
Condition increasing probability of a peril (explosives near building, unhealthy lifestyle)
Risk
Chance or probability of loss resulting from the peril

6.2 Essentials / Characteristics of Insurable Risks

  • Measurable in money — pure sentimental loss cannot be insured.
  • Uncertain — either uncertain whether it will happen (fire, flood) or uncertain when it will happen (death).
  • Fortuitous / Accidental — outside the control of the insured. Deliberate or intentional losses are not insurable.
  • Insurable (not speculative) — stock market speculation, business risks from management decisions are generally not insurable.
  • Sufficient magnitude — very small risks may not be worth insuring; catastrophic risks (nuclear war) may be excluded.
  • Large enough in number — actuarially sound — sufficiently large number of similar risks for accurate prediction.
  • Risk Must Attach — risk must actually run. "If the intended voyage is abandoned for fear of capture, there is no risk by capture."

6.3 Scope of Risk

Scope is determined entirely by the policy. The policy specifies: (1) covered perils; (2) excluded perils (war, nuclear, deliberate acts, inherent vice, wear and tear); (3) subject matter; (4) sum insured; (5) duration.

"All Risks" Policies

Under an all-risks policy, the insurer covers every fortuitous loss not expressly excluded. The burden shifts — the insured does not need to prove the specific cause; the insurer must prove the cause is excluded. Institute Cargo Clauses (ICC) in marine insurance: Clause A = widest cover; Clause C = most restricted.
Gaunt's Case: Wool damaged during transit. Held: Insurer liable under all-risks clause.
Chester's Case: Sponges damaged by dirt and water in transit. Held: Insurer liable under all-risks policy.
Exception: Even in all-risks — natural wear and tear, inherent vices of subject matter are always excluded.

6.5 The Doctrine of Causa Proxima

The Latin Maxim

"Causa proxima non remota spectatur" — "It is the proximate cause, not the remote cause, that should be considered." [causa = cause; proxima = nearest/closest; non = not; remota = remote; spectatur = should be looked at]

A proximate cause is the direct, dominant, operative, and efficient cause of the loss — the cause that sets in motion an unbroken chain of events leading to the loss, without the intervention of any independent cause. It is NOT necessarily the closest in time or the first event in the chain.

Rules of Proximate Cause

⚡
All Rules & Cases — Causa Proxima
▾
RuleCase & Holding
Rule 1: Risk must actually operate In fire insurance: actual ignition must occur. If voyage not taken due to fear of capture — no "capture" — risk does not attach.
Rule 2: Once risk operates, preventive damage is also covered Symington v. Union Insurance — Cork insured against fire; fire broke out nearby; cork thrown into sea to prevent spreading. Held: Insurer liable for BOTH damage from putting out fire AND property thrown into sea — both proximately caused by fire peril.
Rule 3: Novus Actus Interveniens — intervention of new cause breaks chain Marsden v. City Insurance — Fire in building; mob plundered; plate-glass broken. Held: Proximate cause of window breakage = lawlessness of mob (independent cause), NOT the fire. Insurer NOT liable for window. [If goods insured against fire and stolen after fire — proximate cause of theft = theft, not fire. Insurer under fire policy NOT liable.]
Rule 4: Burden of proof Insured must prove proximate cause = covered peril. If insurer relies on an exception — insurer bears the burden of proving the exception applies. Thompson's Case — Fire policy excepted liability if gasoline stored; druggist using gasoline stove for routine cooking; fire broke out. Held: Insurer WAS liable — using stove not "storing" gasoline.
Rule 5: Accident cases — wider interpretation Motor Accident: A injured, moved in dazed condition, fell into water, died of shock. Held: Proximate cause = accident.
Railway Accident: Person injured, got pneumonia from cold exposure. Held: Proximate cause = accident.
Multiple Causes — Lord Dynedin's Rule When multiple causes, one must be selected as proximate using "common sense standard." Tootal v. London Fire Co. — Earthquake caused fire (excluded peril), fire spread by wind and burnt T's building (500 yds away). T had insured against fire. Held: Proximate cause = earthquake (NOT fire). Insurer NOT liable. [Earthquake was the dominant, originating cause.]

§ 55 Marine Insurance Act, 1963 — Proximate Cause in Marine

§ 55(1): "The insurer is liable for any loss proximately caused by a peril insured against... he is not liable for any loss which is not proximately caused by a peril insured against."

§ 55(2) exclusions: Wilful misconduct of assured; loss caused by delay (even if delay caused by covered peril); ordinary wear and tear; inherent vice; rats/vermin; machinery injury not from maritime perils.

⚖ Montoya v. London Assurance Co. — Storm = Proximate Cause of Tobacco Loss

Hides and tobacco shipped and insured. Storm made hides wet; foul smell from wet hides made tobacco unfit. Held: Proximate cause = storm (covered peril). Insurer liable for both hides and tobacco loss.

⚖ Samuel v. Dumas — Wilful Scuttling = Insurer Not Liable

Ship scuttled (deliberately sunk) with owner's connivance; water then flooded in. Held: Dominant reason = scuttling (wilful misconduct). Insurer NOT liable.

7.2 Assignment of Life Insurance — Section 38 (Substituted by Act 5/2015)

📋
Section 38 — Complete Framework
Insurance Act, 1938 · Substituted by Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015
▾

§ 38(1) — How Assignment is Made

Assignment may be made only by: (a) an endorsement upon the policy itself, OR (b) a separate instrument; signed by the assignor (or duly authorised agent) and attested by at least one witness; specifically setting forth the fact of assignment, the reasons, the antecedents of the assignee, and the terms.

§ 38(2) — Insurer's Right to Decline [NEW by 2015 Act]

Insurer may decline to act upon the assignment if it has sufficient reason to believe the transfer is not bona fide, not in the interest of the policyholder, against public interest, or for the purpose of trading of insurance policy (buying and selling policies as financial instruments).

§ 38(3) — Recording Refusal

If insurer refuses — must record reasons in writing and communicate to policyholder within 30 days from date of notice of assignment.

§ 38(4) — Appeal Against Refusal

Person aggrieved may prefer a claim to the Authority (IRDAI) within 30 days of receipt of communication of refusal.

§ 38(5) — When Assignment is Complete

Assignment is complete upon execution of endorsement/instrument duly attested. BUT it is NOT operative as against an insurer, and does NOT confer right to sue, until:

  • A notice in writing of the assignment, AND
  • The endorsement/instrument or a certified copy,

have been delivered to the insurer (at the place where the policy is being serviced).

§ 38(6) — Priority: The Rule in Dearle v. Hall

Priority of claims among competing assignees is determined by the date of notice to the insurer — NOT the date of assignment itself.

⚖ Dearle v. Hall — Rule of Priority

"By giving notice to the insurer a bona fide assignee for value gains priority over earlier assignees who have not done so." For this rule to protect the later assignee: (1) they must have given value; (2) they must have acted in good faith without notice of the earlier assignment (good faith requires actual investigation — passive ignorance is not protected).

§ 38(8) Explanation — Absolute vs. Conditional Assignment

Default: Every assignment is deemed to be an absolute assignment unless the endorsement expressly indicates it is conditional.
§ 38(10) — Conditional Assignment: Valid where proceeds payable to policyholder/nominee if assignee predeceases insured, OR insured survives term. Proviso: a conditional assignee shall NOT obtain a loan on the policy or surrender it.

Assignment and Nomination

A transfer or assignment under § 38 automatically cancels a nomination made under § 39. Exception: assignment to the insurer as security for a loan within surrender value — does NOT cancel nomination, but merely affects nominee's rights to the extent of the insurer's interest.

Warranty — Meaning, Characteristics, Kinds, Effect of Breach & vs. Representation
§§ 35–43 Marine Insurance Act, 1963

8.1 Meaning — The Critical Distinction

Most Important Distinction in Insurance Law

The term "warranty" in insurance law has a meaning completely different from general contract law:

General Contract Law: A warranty is a minor term — its breach gives rise to a claim for damages only, NOT the right to terminate.

Insurance Law: A warranty is a condition of the utmost strictness. Its breach automatically discharges the insurer from liability from the date of breach, regardless of whether the breach caused or was related to the loss.

§ 35(1) Marine Insurance Act, 1963 — Statutory Definition

"A warranty, in the following sections relating to warranties, means a promissory warranty, that is to say a warranty by which the assured undertakes that some particular thing shall or shall not be done, or that some condition shall be fulfilled, or whereby he affirms or negatives the existence of a particular state of facts."

§ 35(3): "A warranty, as above defined, is a condition which must be exactly complied with, whether it be material to the risk or not. If it be not so complied with, then, subject to any express provision in the policy, the insurer is discharged from liability as from the date of the breach of warranty, but without prejudice to any liability incurred by him before that date."

8.2 Characteristics of a Warranty

  • Promissory in Nature — a promise/undertaking for the future, or affirmation of a past/present fact; not merely an opinion.
  • Exact Compliance Required — must be exactly complied with. No room for substantial compliance. "Whether it be material to the risk or not."
  • Materiality Irrelevant — breach discharges the insurer even if the breach was trivial and had no connection with the loss. (E.g., ship warranted crew of 20, carries 19 — insurer discharged even if loss was from a storm.)
  • Discharge from Date of Breach — NOT from date of policy or date of loss. Any liability incurred BEFORE the breach remains.
  • Irremedial — § 36(2): "Where a warranty is broken, the assured cannot avail himself of the defence that the breach has been remedied, and the warranty complied with, before loss." Even full cure before any loss = insurer remains discharged.
  • Express or Implied — warranties may be expressly written into the policy or implied by law.
  • Waiver by Insurer — § 36(3): "A breach of warranty may be waived by the insurer." Accepting premiums after knowing of breach, or continuing to treat policy as in force = waiver.

8.4 Effect of Breach of Warranty

Five Key Consequences — Must Know
  • Automatic Discharge: Insurer automatically discharged from liability upon breach — no election, no notice required. The discharge is instantaneous.
  • From Date of Breach: Not from date of policy or date of loss. Any claims BEFORE the breach date remain payable.
  • Irremedial: Even if breach remedied before loss — insurer remains discharged (§ 36(2)).
  • Materiality Irrelevant: Discharged even if breach had NO causal connection with the loss. This is the most severe and criticized aspect of warranty law.
  • Waiver: Insurer may waive breach (§ 36(3)) — by accepting premiums after knowing of breach, or continuing to treat policy as in force.

8.5 When Breach of Warranty is Excused — § 36(1) MIA 1963

Ground for ExcuseExample
(a) Change of circumstances — warranty ceases to be applicableShip warranted to sail under convoy. Convoy withdrawn because war ends before departure. Warranty of convoy ceases to apply — its purpose (war risk protection) no longer exists.
(b) Compliance with warranty rendered unlawful by subsequent lawShip warranted to sail to a particular port. After policy issued, new law prohibits sailing there. Compliance rendered unlawful — breach is excused.

§ 22(1): Every material representation made by the assured during negotiations must be true — if untrue, insurer may avoid.
