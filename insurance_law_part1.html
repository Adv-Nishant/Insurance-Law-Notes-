I
The Concept & Philosophical Foundation of Insurance

Insurance is humanity's response to uncertainty. The Romans captured this idea in the phrase "homo proponit, sed Deus disponit" — man proposes, God disposes. From the earliest civilizations, human beings recognized that individual misfortune could be catastrophic, but if many shared the burden, no single person would be crushed. This is the social solidarity principle at the heart of insurance.

Riegel & Miller

"It is a social device whereby uncertain risks of individuals may be combined in a group and thus made more certain; small periodic contributions by the individuals providing a fund out of which those who suffer losses may be reimbursed."

Maclean

"Insurance is a method of spreading over a large number of persons a possible financial loss too serious to be conveniently borne by an individual."

Hardy Ivamy — General Principles of Insurance Law

"A contract of insurance is a contract whereby one person called the insurer, undertakes in return for the agreed consideration called the premium, to pay to another person called the insured, a sum of money or its equivalent on the happening of a specified event."

⚖ Prudential Insurance Company v. Inland Revenue Commissioner

Channel J.: "There must be either some uncertainty whether the event will ever happen or not, or if the event is one which must happen at some time or another, there must be uncertainty as to the time at which it will happen."

The two-fold objective of insurance: (i) Immediate — loss/risk/damage spread over large number of risk bearers, immediate beneficiary is the insured; (ii) Far-sighted — accelerates economic growth through investment by insurance companies in the development of commerce and industry.

Ancient Origins
II
Ancient & Medieval Origins — The Global Story

A. Ancient India — Earliest Insurance References

Text
Manusmriti (Laws of Manu)
Text
Yajnavalkya Smriti (Dharmasastra)
Earliest Indian Reference ★
Kautilya's Arthashastra — 4th century BCE
Exam Critical Fact

Kautilya's Arthashastra (4th century BCE) is the earliest known reference to insurance principles in India — pooling of resources for redistribution after fire, floods, epidemics, and famine. Written by Chanakya, minister to Chandragupta Maurya.

B. Ancient Babylon, Greece & Rome

~2250 BCE

Code of Hammurabi (Babylon) — bottomry loans: merchant borrows for sea voyage; if ship lost, loan need not be repaid. This is risk transfer — the very foundation of marine insurance.

Ancient Greece & Rome

Foenus nauticum / nauticum fenus — maritime loans with high interest representing risk premium. Direct ancestor of marine insurance.

Ancient Rome

Roman burial clubs (collegia) — regular contributions to meet funeral expenses of deceased members. Primitive form of life insurance.

C. Medieval Origins — Birth of Formal Marine Insurance

1347 CE

First written marine insurance contracts — Genoa, Italy. Earliest known contract: 23rd October 1347, covering shipment from Genoa to Majorca.

Early 14th c.

Insurance spread to Florence, Venice, Pisa, Barcelona. Recorded in Lex Mercatoria (Law Merchant).

1435

Statute of Barcelona — one of the first codified bodies of insurance law.

1556–1584

Guidon de la Mer — French maritime code with comprehensive marine insurance provisions.

1688

Edward Lloyd's Coffee House, Tower Street, London — gathering place for ship owners, merchants, underwriters. Modern insurance market born here.

D. English Legislative Milestones

1601

Assurance Act (Elizabethan Statute) — Queen Elizabeth I. First formal statutory recognition of marine insurance. Established special chamber of assurance. Marks transition from commercial custom to statutory recognition.

1746

Marine Insurance Act, 1746 (England) — prohibited wagering policies; required genuine insurable interest.

1766

Carter v. Boehm — Lord Mansfield established doctrine of uberrima fides (utmost good faith) as cornerstone of all insurance contracts.

1774

Life Assurance Act, 1774 (England) — also called the Gambling Act. Introduced insurable interest requirement in life insurance to stop gambling on lives of strangers.

1870

British Insurance Act, 1870 — directly influenced insurance law development in India during colonial period.

1906

Marine Insurance Act, 1906 (England) — drafted by Sir Mackenzie Chalmers. Codified centuries of common law on marine insurance. Template for India's Marine Insurance Act, 1963. Remains in force in England.

Lord Mansfield's Contribution

Lord Mansfield, Lord Chief Justice (mid-18th century) — merged Lex Mercatoria (Law Merchant) with common law principles in insurance cases. His judgment in Carter v. Boehm (1766) established uberrima fides as the cornerstone of insurance law.

History in India — Five Stages
III
History of Insurance in India — The Five Stages
STAGE 1 Period of Mushroom Growth 1900 – 1912 ▾

Context: Post Swadeshi Movement (after Partition of Bengal, 1905) — patriotic impulse to boycott British institutions. Led to unregulated mushroom growth of Indian insurance companies — many poorly capitalized, fraudulent.

1818

Oriental Life Insurance Company — established in Calcutta. First life insurance company in India. Failed in 1834. Primarily served Europeans.

1829

Madras Equitable — life insurance in Madras Presidency.

1871

Bombay Mutual (Life Assurance) Society — first genuinely Indian-owned company to survive.

1874

Oriental Insurance Company established.

1897

Empire of India — Bombay Presidency.

1912

Indian Life Assurance Companies Act, 1912 — FIRST STATUTE REGULATING LIFE INSURANCE IN INDIA. Required maintenance of reserves; first supervisory role for government.

1914

Government of India began publishing insurance company returns for the first time.

STAGE 2 Period of Struggle & Steady Growth 1913 – 1938 ▾

Context: Two World Wars; Indian companies struggled against British competitors. Economic turbulence. Weak enforcement allowed malinvestment to continue.

1928

Indian Insurance Companies Act, 1928 — First comprehensive data collection. Extended regulation to general insurance for the first time.

1934

Sri S.C. Sen appointed Special Officer to investigate and report on reform of insurance law.

1936

Sircar Committee (under Sri N.N. Sircar) examined the Sen Report. Recommendations formed direct basis of Insurance Act, 1938.

1938

Insurance Act, 1938 (Act No. 4 of 1938) — enacted 26th February 1938; came into force 1st July 1939. Consolidated and amended the law relating to insurance business. Remains the principal legislation today.

STAGE 3 Period of Stability & Consolidation 1938 – 1950 ▾

Context: Post-1938, Indian companies gained stability. WWII (1939-1945), Indian Independence (1947), and Partition caused massive upheaval.

1945

Cowasji Jehangir Committee — condemned malinvestment by insurance companies (investing policyholder funds in speculative ventures).

1946

Insurance Act, 1938 amended — custody and inspection provisions strengthened.

1947

Indian Independence. Partition disrupted many companies headquartered in what became Pakistan.

1950

Insurance Amendment Act, 1950 (Act 47 of 1950) — based on Sri S.R. Ranganathan Committee review. Far-reaching changes: abolished principal agencies; stricter capital requirements; tightened investment regulations; extended Act to co-operative and provident societies.

STAGE 4 Period of Boom & Nationalization 1950 onwards ▾

Background to Life Insurance Nationalization: Planned economy philosophy; mobilize domestic savings; channel funds into government securities; extend coverage; eliminate exploitative practices. Trigger: financial scandal — companies had massively misappropriated policyholder funds.

19 Jan 1956

Government of India issued Ordinance nationalizing life insurance sector. This is the date of nationalization by ordinance.

1 Sep 1956

Life Insurance Corporation of India (LIC) established under Life Insurance Corporation Act, 1956 (Act 31 of 1956). 245 companies nationalized — 154 Indian insurers + 16 non-Indian insurers + 75 Provident Societies.

1968

Insurance Act amended — investment regulation; Tariff Advisory Committee established under Part IIB.

1971

General Insurance Corporation of India (GIC) incorporated under Companies Act.

1 Jan 1973

General Insurance Business (Nationalization) Act, 1972 (Act 57 of 1972) effective. 107 insurers nationalized, grouped into 4 GIC subsidiaries.

The Four GIC Subsidiaries — Must Know
  • National Insurance Company Limited — headquartered in Kolkata
  • New India Assurance Company Limited — headquartered in Mumbai
  • Oriental Insurance Company Limited — headquartered in New Delhi
  • United India Insurance Company Limited — headquartered in Chennai
LIC Act, 1956 — Key Provisions

Section 4 (Composition): Not more than 16 persons appointed by Central Government, one as Chairman.
Section 5 (Capital): Original capital — ₹5 crores provided by Central Government.
Section 6 (Functions): Carry on life insurance business in and outside India; develop life insurance to best advantage of the community. Motto: "Yogakshemam Vahamyaham" (I bear the responsibility of your welfare).

STAGE 5 Era of Privatization 1991 onwards ▾

Context: 1991 balance of payments crisis; LPG reforms under Finance Minister Dr. Manmohan Singh and PM P.V. Narasimha Rao opened door to privatization.

1993

Malhotra Committee (formally: Committee on Reforms in Insurance Sector) — chaired by R.N. Malhotra, former Governor of Reserve Bank of India.

1994

Malhotra Committee Report submitted. Key recommendations: allow private sector; foreign companies as joint ventures; FDI up to 26%; create independent regulatory authority.

1999

IRDA Act, 1999 (Act 41 of 1999) — Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority constituted.

Apr 2000

IRDAI incorporated and became operational.

Aug 2000

Market opened — registration invitations; foreign companies allowed up to 26% FDI.

Dec 2000

GIC subsidiaries restructured as independent companies; GIC converted into national re-insurer.

Jul 2002

Parliament passed bill de-linking 4 subsidiaries from GIC — complete independence.

2015

Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015 (Act 5 of 2015) — FDI raised to 49%; Section 45 changed from 2 to 3 years; major amendments to nomination, assignment, registration.

2019

Budget 2019 — FDI limit raised to 100%.

2021

Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021 — modified FDI provisions (amended S. 2(7A)(b)).

Critical Section Numbers — Must Know

SectionSubjectKey Content
§ 2(1A) "Authority" The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI)
§ 2(6A) "Fire insurance business" Business of effecting contracts of insurance against loss by or incidental to fire
§ 2(6B) "General insurance business" Fire, marine, or miscellaneous insurance business
§ 2(6C) "Health insurance business" Inserted by Act 5 of 2015
§ 2(9) "Insurer" Substituted by Act 5 of 2015: (a) Indian Insurance Company; (b) Statutory body by Parliament; (c) Insurance co-operative; (d) Foreign company doing re-insurance through India branch
§ 2(11) "Life insurance business" Business of effecting contracts of insurance upon human life
§ 2(13A) "Marine insurance business" Insurance on vessels, cargoes, freights, transit by land or water
§ 2(13B) "Miscellaneous insurance business" Insurance not principally or wholly fire, life or marine
§ 2(16B) "Re-insurance" Insurance of part of one insurer's risk by another insurer for a mutually acceptable premium
§ 2C Prohibition — certain persons Only (a) public company, (b) co-operative society, or (c) foreign body corporate (not private) can carry on insurance business. From IRDA Act 1999: only Indian insurance companies can begin new business.
§ 3 Registration Certificate of registration from Authority required before commencing or continuing any class of insurance business. S. 3(2A): Grounds for granting registration — financial condition, adequate capital, public interest, compliance.
§ 3A Annual Fee Insurer shall pay annual fee to Authority as specified by regulations. Failure renders registration liable to cancellation.
§ 6 Capital Requirements Life / General: ₹100 crore paid-up equity; Health: ₹100 crore; Re-insurance: ₹200 crore; Foreign re-insurer branch: net owned funds ₹5,000 crore (S. 6(2)); IFSC branch: ₹1,000 crore (S. 6(3), ins. Act 23/2019)
§ 10 Separation of Accounts Insurer carrying on more than one class must keep separate accounts. Life insurance fund (S. 10(2)) — absolutely the security of life policyholders; assets kept distinct and separate.
§ 13 Actuarial Report Life insurer: actuarial investigation at least once per year into financial condition, including valuation of liabilities.
§ 38 Assignment & Transfer Substituted by Act 5/2015. By endorsement on policy itself or separate instrument, signed by assignor, attested by at least one witness. Insurer may refuse if not bona fide or against public interest.
§ 39 Nomination Substituted by Act 5/2015. S. 39(7)&(8): where nominee is spouse, parent, or child — beneficial entitlement. Overruled (partially) Sarbati Devi v. Usha Devi (nominee = mere receiver).
§ 45 Incontestability Substituted by Act 5/2015. Policy cannot be questioned after 3 years (changed from 2 years). Within 3 years: challengeable on fraud or misstatement of material facts affecting life expectancy.
§ 64VA Sufficiency of Assets (Solvency) Every insurer shall at all times maintain excess of assets over liabilities of not less than 50% of minimum capital under § 6. Failure = deemed insolvent.
§ 64VB Advance Premium No risk to be assumed unless premium received in advance. Cheque/money order: risk attaches on date of posting.
§ 101A Re-insurance — Indian Re-insurers Every insurer shall re-insure with Indian re-insurers. Maximum re-insurance: 30% of sum assured on each policy.
§ 114A IRDAI Regulations Power of IRDAI to make regulations. Inserted by Act 41/1999.

Functions of IRDAI (Section 14, IRDA Act 1999)

  • 01Issuing, renewing, modifying, withdrawing, suspending or cancelling registrations of insurers and intermediaries.
  • 02Protecting policyholder interests — including complaints redressal.
  • 03Specifying qualifications, code of conduct, and training for intermediaries and agents.
  • 04Specifying the code of conduct for surveyors and loss assessors.
  • 05Promoting efficiency in the conduct of insurance businesses.
  • 06Promoting and regulating professional organizations connected with insurance and re-insurance.
  • 07Levying fees and other charges.
  • 08Inspecting and investigating insurers, intermediaries, and other relevant organizations.
  • 09Regulating rates, advantages, terms, and conditions not covered by the Tariff Advisory Committee under Section 64U.
  • 10Specifying how books should be kept.
  • 11Regulating company investment of funds.
  • 12Regulating a margin of solvency.
  • 13Adjudicating disputes between insurers and intermediaries.
  • 14Supervising the Tariff Advisory Committee.
  • 15Specifying the percentage of premium income to finance schemes for promoting professional organizations.
  • 16Specifying the percentage of life and general insurance business to be undertaken in rural or social sectors.
  • 17Specifying the form and manner in which books of accounts shall be maintained by insurers.
Social Obligation Provisions (Liberalized Era)

Section 32B (ins. by Act 41/1999): Every insurer shall undertake specified percentages of life and general insurance business in rural and social sectors.
Section 32C: Insurers shall provide insurance policies to persons in rural areas, workers in unorganized/informal sectors, economically vulnerable classes — including insurance for crops.

Five Stages Summary — Examination Format
  • Stage 1 (1900–1912): Mushroom Growth — Swadeshi movement → Indian Life Assurance Companies Act, 1912 (first regulatory statute); returns published from 1914.
  • Stage 2 (1913–1938): Struggle & Steady Growth — Indian Insurance Companies Act 1928 (first data collection for general insurance); Sen (1934), Sircar Committee (1936); Insurance Act 1938.
  • Stage 3 (1938–1950): Stability & Consolidation — Cowasji Jehangir Committee (1945); S.R. Ranganathan review; Amendment Act 1950 (abolished principal agencies).
  • Stage 4 (1950 onwards): Boom & Nationalization — LIC ordinance 19 Jan 1956; LIC Act 1956 (245 companies); GIC 1972 (107 companies, 4 subsidiaries); GIBNA effective 1 Jan 1973.
  • Stage 5 (1991 onwards): Privatization — Malhotra Committee 1993; IRDA Act 1999; IRDAI operational Apr 2000; market opened Aug 2000; FDI 26%→49%→100%.
Case Law References — Topic 1
  • Carter v. Boehm (1766) — Lord Mansfield; established doctrine of uberrima fides (utmost good faith) as cornerstone of all insurance contracts.
  • Prudential Insurance Company v. Inland Revenue Commissioner — Channel J.; judicial definition of insurance contract (uncertainty as to happening/time of event).
  • Sarbati Devi v. Usha Devi — Supreme Court; nominee under § 39 is merely "the hand to receive" — not beneficial owner. (Modified by Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015 via § 39(7) and (8).)
